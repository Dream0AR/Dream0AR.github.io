《微分几何初步》1.6-1.7 "Preliminary Differential Geometry" 1.6-1.7

Partial standard type ---------- We know that when studying the local properties of curves, it is much more convenient to use the Frenet frame. Take (no first-order singularity) arc length parameterized curve $\alpha:I\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^3,$ Expanded by Taylor, $\alpha(s)=\alpha(0)+s\alpha'(0)+\frac{s^2}{2}\alpha''(0)+\frac{s^3}{6}\alpha'''(0)+R,$ $R=o(s^3).$ We know $\alpha'(0)=t,$ $\alpha''(0)=kn,$ $\alpha'''(0)=(kn)'=k'n+kn'=k'n-k^2t-k\tau b.$ After finishing, we get $\alpha(s)-\alpha(0)=(s-\frac{k^2s^3}{6})t+(\frac{s^2k}{2}+\frac{s^3k'}{6})n-\frac{s^3}{6}k\tau b+R,$ Thus we obtain the (high-order) local approximate coordinates of the curve under the Frenet frame: $$\left\{ \begin{aligned} x(s)&=s-\frac{k^2s^3}{6}+R_x\\ y(s)&=\frac{k}{2}s^2+\frac{k's^3}{6}+R_y\\ z(s)&=-\frac{k\tau}{6}s^3+R_z \end{aligned} \right.$$ The above formula is called the local standard form of the curve $\alpha$ in the neighborhood of the zero point. Viewed from a first-order perspective, Only the tangent vector $t,$ can be seen, that is, only the information in the $x$ direction; and from a third-order perspective, You can see more information in $\mathbb{R}^3$, such as the direction of the curve ($n$ direction), Climb ($b$ direction) and so on. From this we can see some geometric properties: When the torsion is negative, Locally, the curve goes up through the osculating plane; while the curve with positive torsion goes down. From the tangent plane (rectifying), the curve can be divided on one side in a certain neighborhood $(k\neq 0).$ An important piece of information about the oblique plane is that it is the closest (most dense) plane to the curve. In mathematical language, that is, It is the limit of the plane determined by the tangent line at point $s$ and point $s+h$ when $h\rightarrow 0$. Let’s set $s=0,$ using Frenet frame and local standard form, This plane can be recorded as $z=cy$ (excluded from the tangent plane $y=0$). Then calculate $c=\frac{z(h)}{y(h)}=\frac{-\frac{k\tau}{6}h^3+o(h^3)}{\frac{k}{2}h^2+o(h^2)}=-\frac{\tau h}{3}+o(h).$ Therefore, the plane tends to $z=0,$, which is the close plane. **Note 1.1**. *Some books may define torsion as $b'=-\tau n,$. Please note.* Let’s do some exercises. We mentioned that from the tangent plane, the curve can be divided on one side in a neighborhood of point $P$, In fact, any plane containing a tangent to point $P$ can be used, except close planes. Just consider the projection of the curve along the $x$ axis (tangential direction) on the normal plane, Then the previous $z=cy$ is the secant line between the zero point and $h$ point of the curve, and the slope of the secant line tends to zero. So for any given plane $z=c_0y,$ $c_0\neq 0,$ It is always possible to find a small enough neighborhood so that the secant slope is less than $c_0,$ Therefore, the projected curve remains below the $z=c_0 y$ straight line, that is, the original curve remains on one side of the plane. The close plane can also be determined in other ways, For example, the limit of the plane determined by the three points $\alpha(s),\alpha(s+h_1),\alpha(s+h_2)$ is $h_1,h_2\rightarrow 0$. Also take the local standard form, then the plane can be recorded as $ax+by+cz=0.$ Take $F(s)=ax(s)+by(s)+cz(s),$ then $F'(0)=a,$ $F''(0)=bk.$ Here $a,b$ are functions about $h_1,h_2$. Since $F(0),F(h_1),F(h_2)=0,$ From the mean value theorem, we know that $a,b\rightarrow 0.$, so the plane tends to $z=0,$, which is a close plane. A classic conclusion is that the curvature of the curve at point $P$ is consistent with the curvature of the close plane curve projected at point $P$ at that point. This is because from the local standard type, The curvature of $P$ point$(s=0)$ can be determined by $\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{2y(s)}{x^2(s)}$, It has nothing to do with $z$. Overall properties of plane curves ---------------- Next, we discuss the global differential properties of some plane curves. Chapter 5 will discuss them more systematically, Interested students can take a look later. Do some simple review, Plane closed curve $\alpha:[a,b]\rightarrow R^2$ while requiring $\alpha(a)=\alpha(b)$, It also requires the higher order derivatives to be consistent. It is **simple** if the closed curve does not self-intersect. Generally, the arc length is parameterized, and the curvature of the plane curve will have positive and negative signs. We say that a simple closed curve in the plane will frame an internal region, which can be obtained from the Jordan Curve Theorem, See Chapter 5. If you look in the direction in which the curve parameters increase, the interior always remains on the left side of the curve. Then the curve is said to be **positively oriented**. ### Equal period inequalities This is the oldest overall problem in differential geometry. When do simple closed curves of equal length enclose the largest area? Weierstrass gave the earliest proof of the variational method, but it was rather cumbersome. Here we introduce the proof given by Schmidt in 1939. For the area enclosed by the curve, according to Green's formula, We have the formula for calculating area: $$A=-\int_{a}^b yx'dt=\int_a^bxy'dt=\frac{1}{2}\int_a^b xy'-yx'dt.$$ Or as the book proves through elementary methods, but the method is essentially similar to the elementary proof of Green's formula, And need to admit some conclusions (can find a straight line, Such that the distance function of the curve with respect to the straight line has a finite number of extreme points). **Theorem 1.2** (Isoperipheral inequality). *Suppose $C$ is a plane simple closed curve with length $l$, $A$ is the area enclosed by $C$, then $l^2-4\pi A\ge 0,$ The equality sign is true if and only if $C$ is the circumference.* **Certificate:** As shown in the figure, take appropriate parallel lines $L, L'$ and clamp the curve $C$. Take a circle $S^1$ with a radius $r$ below that is also stuck, with the center of the circle as the origin, The vertical $L, L'$ direction is the $x$ axis, and the $L, L'$ direction is the $y$ axis. Parameterize the arc length of $C$ $\alpha(s)=(x(s),y(s)).$ Suppose $S^1$ curve is $\bar\alpha(s)=(x(s),\bar y(s)),$ that is, the two curves in $x$ direction are synchronized. From the area formula, we have: $$\begin{aligned} A+\pi r^2=\int_0^l(xy'-\bar yx')ds\le \int_0^l\sqrt{x^2+\bar y^2} \cdot \sqrt{ {y'}^2+{x'}^2}ds=lr. \end{aligned}$$ Thus, the geometric mean $\sqrt{A}\sqrt{\pi r^2}\le \frac{1}{2}(A+\pi r^2)\le \frac{1}{2}lr,$ $4 \pi A r^2\le l^2 r^2,$ is $l^2-4\pi A\ge 0.$ (note that it has nothing to do with $r$.) When the equal sign holds, all inequalities are equal. From the geometric mean inequality, $A=\pi r^2.$ Therefore, $l=2\pi r,$ and any rotation of $L, L'$ direction, the distance is still $2r$. At the same time $(x,\bar y)=\lambda (y',-x'),$ we know from the module length relationship $|\lambda|=r,$ Thus $x=\pm r y'.$ Rotate $L,L'$ by $90$ degrees, and get $y=\pm r x'.$ So $x^2+y^2=r^2({x'}^2+{y'}^2)=r^2,$ that is, $C$ is the circumference of the circle. **Note 1.3**. *It is easy to see that the equal period inequality holds for the simple closed curve $C^1$, and it also naturally holds for the segmented $C^1$. (When not specified, the default is smooth.)* ### Four vertex theorem Closed curve parameterized for arc length $\alpha(s)=(x(s),y(s)),$ Introduce the tangent line $t(s)=(x'(s),y'(s)).$ and its trajectory falls on the unit circle, And the velocity vector is $kn$. Let $\theta(s)$ be the intersection angle between $t(s)$ and $x$ axis, If it is restricted to fall on $[0,2\pi)$, then $\theta$ may not be continuous. Since it can be defined locally by inverse trigonometric functions $(\theta(s)=\arctan\frac{y'(s)}{x'(s)}(+\pi)),$ It is differentiable. From $t(s)=(\cos \theta(s),\sin \theta(s)),$ velocity vector $t'=\theta' n,$ That is $\theta'=k.$ Thus the continuous function $\theta(s)=\int_0^s k(s)ds.$ can be redefined as a whole It differs from the original function by $2k\pi.$ This function describes the total rotation angle of the tangent vector, Since the tangent vectors at the beginning and end of the closed curve are the same, the rotation angle is an integer multiple of $2\pi$. Remember $\int_0^l k(s)ds=\theta(l)-\theta(0)=2\pi I,$ Call the integer $I$ the **rotation index** of the curve $\alpha$. Note that since $k$ is signed, The same goes for the rotation indicator $I$. When the simple closed curve has a positive orientation, the rotation indicator is also positive. We have the following theorem, which is proved in Theorem 2 of Section 5.7: **Theorem 1.4** (Rotation Index Theorem). *The rotation index of a simple closed curve is $\pm 1,$ The sign depends on the orientation of the curve.* Next, the concept of curve vertices is introduced. The regular plane curve $\alpha:[a,b]\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ is said to be **convex**, If $\,\forall\,t\in [a,b], the trajectory of $ $\alpha$ always falls on the tangent side of point $t$. The **vertex** of a plane curve is defined as all points $t$ such that $k'(t)=0$, That is, the global stationary point of curvature (some books will define it more accurately as an extreme point). For example, An ellipse has exactly four vertices. Interestingly, any simple convex closed curve has at least four vertices. First prove a lemma. **Lemma 1.5**. *Denote $\alpha(s)=(x(s),y(s)):[0,l]\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ as the arc length parameterized plane closed curve. Then $\,\forall\,A,B,C\in \mathbb{R},$ $\int_0^l(Ax+By+C)k'ds=0.$* **Certificate:** The $\theta$ function defined at the beginning, We have $(x',y')=(\cos \theta,\sin \theta),$ $k=\theta'$ and $x''=-ky',$ $y''=kx'.$ Therefore, for a closed curve, there are $\int_0^l k'ds=0,$ $\int_0^l xk'ds=-\int_0^lkx'ds=-\int_0^ly''ds=0,$ $\int_0^l yk'ds=-\int_0^lky'ds=\int_0^lx''ds=0.$ Thus the lemma is proved. **Theorem 1.6** (Four Vertices Theorem). *A simple convex closed curve has at least four vertices.* **Proof:** To parameterize the arc length of the curve, we only need to examine the behavior of the continuous function $k(s)$ on $[0,l]$. Since it is a periodic function, the maximum value point and the minimum value point are both stationary points. Therefore, we have found two vertices $p, q.$. These two points divide the arcs at both ends, and draw a straight line $L$ through $p, q$, First, it needs to be stated that each arc remains on one side of $L$. Otherwise, assuming that a certain arc also intersects $L$ at point $r$, then for the three points $p, q, r$ on $L$, The middle point may be selected as $p$. The tangent line of point $p$ must be exactly $L$, otherwise $q, r$ are on both sides of the tangent line. Contradictory to convexity. However, even if the tangent to point $p$ is $L$, The tangent line near point $p$ will still divide $q, r$ on both sides of the tangent line, which is inconsistent with convexity. Unless $p$ remains a straight line near point $q, r$. At this time, $k=0$ at the maximum point of $p, q$, This shows that $k\equiv 0$ cannot be a closed curve. On the other hand, if the two end arcs remain on the same side of $L$, then a certain arc can be degenerated into a straight line due to the similarity of convexity, The same contradiction occurs. Therefore, the two end arcs are on both sides of $L$. Let $Ax+By+C=0$ be the equation of the straight line $L$. If there are no other vertices, then $k'$ is always negative and always positive on the two arcs respectively. Furthermore, the sign can be adjusted so that $(Ax+By+C)k'$ is always positive except for $p, q$ points on $C$. This is contradictory to the previous lemma, Thus, it can be found that the third vertex is located on a certain arc, and $k'$ changes sign at this point. Since the arc connects the minimum and maximum points of $k(s)$, the sign of $k'(s)$ changes an even number of times, Therefore, the fourth vertex must be found on this arc. The four-vertex theorem has been the subject of many studies. In fact, the theorem is true for simple closed curves. But the proof is a little more complicated, you can check the references. I have always mentioned the *simple convex* closed curve, But in fact it is somewhat redundant. Proposition 1 of Section 5.7 will prove that a plane closed curve is convex if and only if it is simple, And there is an orientation such that the curvature is non-negative. Therefore, the four-vertex theorem can be expressed more accurately as follows: **Theorem 1.7** (Four Vertex Theorem'). *There is an orientation such that the curvature function of a closed convex curve is non-negative, and there are (strictly) at least two maximum points and minimum points, or the curvature is constant (in this case, the curve is a circle).* A natural question is, for any given periodic function $k:[a,b]\rightarrow \mathbb{R},$ If it satisfies the conclusion of the four vertex theorem, Is it possible to find a simple closed curve such that $k$ becomes its curvature function? That is the converse of the four-vertex theorem. This is similar to the fundamental theorem of curve theory in three-dimensional space. For the case where the function is strictly positive, H.Gluck gave a positive answer in 1971. B.Dahlberg obtained the complete inverse theorem in 1997. The topic of the four-vertex theorem and its inverse theorem was edited and integrated into a very detailed paper in 2005. Interested students can check it out. ### Cauchy-Crofton formula We first give this interesting theorem directly, and then only give the proof of the theorem: **Theorem 1.8** (Cauchy-Crofton formula). *Let $C$ be a plane regular curve with length $l$, then the overall measure of all straight lines (with multiplicity) that have intersections with $C$ is $2l.$* Two points need to be explained here. First, the multiplicity refers to the number of intersections between the straight line and the curve $C$. That is, if a straight line has $n$ intersection points with curve $C$, then the straight line is recorded $n$ times in the set. Secondly, we need to explain what the measure is. Take the unit vector perpendicular to the straight line $v=(\cos \theta,\sin \theta)$, Let $p$ be the inner product of any point on the straight line and $v$, then the straight line is completely determined by these two parameters. Denote the entire straight line as $\mathcal{L}=\{(p,\theta)\in \mathbb{R}^2; (p,\theta)\sim (-p,\theta+\pi)\},$ We show that a reasonable measure can be uniquely defined on it. Reasonable here means that we hope that the measure remains unchanged under rigid transformation. First, there is the following simple proposition: **Proposition 1.9**. *Denote $f(x,y)$ as a continuous function on $\mathbb{R}^2$, and define its area $A(S)=\iint_S f(x,y)dxdy for the set $S\subset \mathbb{R}^2,$. $ If $A$ is a rigid transformation invariant, then $f$ must be a constant.* **Proof:** Let the rigid transformation be $F$. Since the Jacobian of the rigid transformation is $1$, We have $A(F(S))=\iint_S f(F(x,y))dxdy=\iint_S f(x,y)dxdy=A(S).$ Since it holds for any $S$, $f\circ F=f.$ is transferable in $\mathbb{R}^2$ due to rigid transformation, That is $\,\forall\,p,q\in \mathbb{R}^2,$ $\,\exists\,$ Rigidly transform $F$ so that $F(p)=q,$ means $f=\text{const}.$ For the curve $x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta=p,$ in $\mathcal{L}$ Its rigid transformation$\begin{cases} \bar x=a+x\cos \varphi -y\sin \varphi\\ \bar y=b+x\sin \varphi +y\cos \varphi \end{cases}$, the parameters become $\begin{cases} \bar \theta=\theta + \varphi\\ \bar p=p+a\cos(\theta+\varphi)+b\sin(\theta+\varphi) \end{cases}.$ It is easy to see that the Jacobian of this transformation under $(p,\theta)$ coordinates is also $1,$ At the same time, this effect is obviously transferable on the entire straight line $\mathcal{L}$, Therefore, in the same way, it can be seen that the measure can be uniquely defined as $\mu(\mathcal{G})=\iint_\mathcal{G} dpd\theta$, This measure is called a kinematic measure. Next we give the general idea of proving the theorem. **Proof:** For $l$ long straight line segment $C,$ since the measure is invariant under rigid transformation, Let’s assume that $C$ is centered on the origin and falls on the $x$ axis. Then the measure of the desired set is: $$\int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{|\cos\theta|l/2}dpd\theta=\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{l}{2}|\cos\theta |d\theta=2l.$$ Next, for a polyline $C composed of a finite number of line segments, $ sum the measures of the straight lines corresponding to each line segment, It can be obtained that the measure of the desired set with multiplicity is $\iint n(p,\theta)dpd\theta=2\sum_i l_i=2l.$ Finally, taking the limit, we can expect that the above formula is applicable to any regular curve, which proves the theorem. One thing to note is that for straight line segments, there will actually be straight lines with infinite multiplicity. But it measures zero under the kinematic measure. For the proof in the book, It is cumbersome to describe the process of taking the limit precisely. A more concrete proof is as follows: **Proof:** For the straight line corresponding to $(p,\theta)$, It can also be converted into the more common method of first determining the point $Z(s)=(x(s),y(s))\in C,$ and then determining the angle $\eta.$ That is, convert $(p,\theta)$ coordinates to $(s,\eta)$ coordinates. Here let $\theta=\eta,$ The geometric meaning is the same. Then $p=x(s)\cos \eta+y(s)\sin \eta.$ Note that $(x',y')$ can be expressed as $(\cos\phi,\sin \phi),$ Then the Jacobian is $|\cos(\phi(s)-\eta)|.$ Therefore, according to Fubini's theorem, the required measure is: $$\begin{aligned} \int_{\{L:L\cap C\neq \varnothing\} }ndpd\theta&=\int_{\{L:L\cap C\neq \varnothing\} }(\sum_{Z\in L\cap C}1)dpd\theta\\ &=\int_{\{Z:Z\in C\} }\int_{\{L:Z\in L\} }dpd\theta\\ &=\int_0^{l}\int_{0}^{\pi} |\cos(\phi(s)-\eta)|d\eta ds\\ &=\int_0^l 2 ds=2l. \end{aligned}$$ The basic ideas of this topic belong to the branch of Integral Geometry. An interesting application of the Cauchy-Crofton formula is to estimate curve lengths. Take a family of straight lines with equal spacing $r$, and then rotate them at equal angles $\alpha$ across the entire plane, Then $l=\frac{1}{2}\int ndpd\theta\approx \frac{1}{2}nr\alpha,$ $n$ is the total number of intersection points (number of points) between the curve and these straight lines, that is, the integral is discretized.
