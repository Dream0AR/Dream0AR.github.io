vector field ------ ### Vector field on the plane The **vector field** on the open set $U\subset \mathbb{R}^2$ is a map, For each $q=(x,y)\in U$, specify a $w(q)=(a(x,y),b(x,y))\in \mathbb{R}^2.$ The vector field is said to be **differentiable**, if $w$ is differentiable, that is, $a, b$ are both differentiable. By default we always consider differentiable vector fields. Given a vector field, we consider whether there is a locus of the field, That is, whether there is a differentiable curve $\alpha(t)=(x(t),y(t)),$ Such that $\alpha'(t)=w(\alpha(t)).$ In fact, this is the solution to the system of ordinary differential equations: $$\left\{ \begin{aligned} \frac{dx}{dt}=a(x,y)\\ \frac{dy}{dt}=b(x,y) \end{aligned} \right.$$ We say that the vector field $w$ determines the above system of differential equations. For example, for the vector field $w(x,y)=(x,y),$ The trajectory passing through $(x_0,y_0)$ is $\alpha(t)=(x_0e^t,y_0 e^t);$ For the vector field $w(x,y)=(y,-x),$ The trajectory passing through $(x_0,y_0)$ is $\beta(t)=(r\sin t,r\cos t),$ $r^2=x_0^2+y_0^2.$ From the basic theory of ordinary differential equations (existence, uniqueness), we have the following theorem: **Theorem 1.1**. *For a given vector field $w,$ and a fixed point $p\in U,$ on any open set $U\subset \mathbb{R}^2$, there is a trajectory of the field $\alpha:I\rightarrow U,$ where $I$ is the small neighborhood at the origin, $\alpha(0)=p. $ Such a trajectory is unique, that is, if there is another trajectory $\beta:J\rightarrow U,$ $J$ is a small neighborhood of the origin, $\beta(0)=p,$ is limited to $I\cap J$$\alpha=\beta.$* Based on the dependence of the solution on the initial value, we further have: **Theorem 1.2**. *The meaning of the letters is the same as above, there is $p$ point neighborhood $V\subset U\subset \mathbb{R}^2,$ and there is a differentiable mapping $\alpha:V\times I\rightarrow U,$ such that $\,\forall\,q\in V,$ $\alpha(q,\cdot)$ is the trajectory of field $w$ through $q$ point.* Such a mapping $\alpha$ is called the (local) **flow** (local flow) of $w$ at point $p$. From the diagram, It "shoots" the cylinder onto the plane $(U)$, mapping each vertical line as a trajectory. From the above theorem, the conclusion is as follows: **Lemma 1.3**. *Let $w$ be a vector field on the open set $U\subset \mathbb{R}^2$, $p\in U$ has $w(p)\neq 0.$ Then there exists $p$ point neighborhood $W\subset U$ and a differentiable function $f:W\rightarrow \mathbb{R},$ such that $f$ is constant along the trajectory, and $df_q\neq 0,$ $\,\forall\,q\in W.$* **Proof:** Let $p$ be the origin, and $w(p)$ be along the direction of $x$ axis. From the above theorem we have local flow $\alpha:V\times I\rightarrow U.$ Take $\tilde\alpha$ as $\alpha$ and limit it to the rectangle $(V\times I)\cap \{(0,y,t)\in \mathbb{R}^3\}$. By definition, $d\tilde \alpha_p$ maps the tangent direction of the $t$ axis to $w(p),$ Reflect the tangent direction of the $y$ axis to itself, so $d\tilde \alpha_p$ does not degenerate. According to the inverse function theorem, On a sufficiently small neighborhood $W\subset U$, there exists a differentiable function $\tilde \alpha^{-1}.$ Then take $f$ as the component of $\tilde \alpha^{-1}$ on the $y$ axis. It is easy to see that $f$ meets the requirements. Such a function $f$ that is constant along the trajectory (solution) is called the (local) first integral of the field $w$ in the neighborhood of point $p$. For example, for the field $w(x,y)=(y,-x),$ A first integral on $\mathbb{R}^2-O$ is $f(x,y)=x^2+y^2.$ It is easy to see that the first integral defined in this way is not unique. According to the implicit function theorem, where the differential of $f$ does not disappear, $f=C$ gives the relationship between $x, y$, thereby eliminating an unknown quantity and making the solution easier. The **direction field**$r,$ on the open set $U\subset \mathbb{R}^2$ refers to each point $p\in U,$ Specify a straight line $r(p).$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ that passes through point $p$. $ is said to be differentiable at point $p$, If there is a non-zero differentiable vector field $w,$ on $p$ point neighborhood $V\subset U$ Satisfying $\,\forall\,q\in V$, non-zero $w(q)$ is the basis of $r(q)$. The fact that $r$ is differentiable on $U$ naturally means that it is slightly differentiable on $U$. Each non-zero differentiable vector field $w$ can naturally generate a differentiable direction field $r.$ It should be noted that, according to the definition, each differentiable direction field point locally corresponds to a non-zero differentiable vector field $w,$ But it does not necessarily correspond to a non-zero differentiable vector field as a whole, as shown in the figure below. It can be said that the vector field corresponding to the direction field locally gives an orientation of the direction field, Then a direction field that cannot be oriented as a whole cannot correspond to an overall non-zero vector field. A regular curve $C\subset U$ is called the **integral curve** of the direction field $r$ above $U$, If $\,\forall\,q\in C,$ $r(q)$ is the tangent line of $C$ at point $q$. It is easy to see that the integral curve is locally the trajectory of the vector field corresponding to the differentiable direction field, Therefore, each differentiable direction field can always find the integral curve passing through any fixed point, That is, similar to the splicing of solutions in ordinary differential equations, the integral curve is to splice the trajectories of the vector fields together. Since the trajectory and the integral curve seem to correspond to the same thing, why introduce a new definition? Because the overall direction field does not necessarily correspond to the overall vector field, there is no concept of the so-called overall trajectory. But the overall integral curve does exist. It can be seen that differentiability is also very important for the direction field. By default we always consider differentiable direction fields. The direction field can also be given by a differential equation. For any vector field $w,$ It is easy to see that the direction fields generated by $\,\forall\,\lambda\neq 0,$ $\lambda w$ and $w$ are consistent, $\lambda$ can be a differentiable function that is non-zero everywhere. For a system of ordinary differential equations $\begin{cases} \frac{dx}{dt}=\lambda(x,y)a(x,y)\\ \frac{dy}{dt}=\lambda(x,y)b(x,y) \end{cases},$ In order to eliminate $\lambda,$ This direction field can be expressed by the differential equation $b(x,y)\frac{dx}{dt}-a(x,y)\frac{dy}{dt}=0$, The solution to the equation corresponds to the integral curve. For the direction field, we don’t care about the parameterization of the curve (for the trajectory of the vector field, we need to pay attention to parameter selection), Therefore, we often use differential equations of the form $adx+bdy=0$ to represent the direction field. ### Vector field on surface All the previous contents only use the local properties on $\mathbb{R}^2$, mainly relying on its differentiable structure. Therefore, we can naturally transfer the above discussion to the surface. **Definition 1.4**. *The vector field $w,$ on the open set $U$ in the surface $S$ specifies a correspondence of $w(p)\in T_p(S)$ for each point $p\in U,$. It is said to be differentiable at $p$ point. If there are differentiable $a(u,v), b(u,v),$ for parameterized $x(u,v),$ in a neighborhood of $p$ point, such that $w(x(u,v))=a(u,v)x_u+b(u,v)x_v.$ It has nothing to do with parameterization selection.* Similarly, you can define the trajectory of the vector field, the direction field, the integral curve, etc. Just replace $\mathbb{R}^2$ with $S$, and the theorem and lemma are naturally established. Next, we introduce the main theorem of this section. **Theorem 1.5**. *On the open set $U\subset S$, there are two vector fields $w_1, w_2,$ that are linearly independent at a certain point $p\in U$. Then there is a certain $p$ point neighborhood $V\subset U,$ on which there is parameterization such that $\,\forall\,q\in V,$ The two coordinate curves passing through the point are tangent to $w_1(q), w_2(q)$ respectively.* **Proof:** Take the $p$ point neighborhood $W\subset U,$ so that it has the first integral $f_1,f_2.$ Definition $\varphi:W\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2,$ $\varphi(q)=(f_1(q),f_2(q)),$ Then $d\varphi_p(w_1)=(0,(df_2)_p(w_1))\neq 0,$ $d\varphi_p(w_2)=((df_1)_p(w_2),0)\neq 0,$ and linearly independent, $d\varphi_p$ does not degenerate. Therefore $\varphi$ is a local homeomorphism, Therefore, $\varphi$ can be taken as the coordinate function on the smaller neighborhood $V\subset W$. The coordinate curves $f_1(q)=const, f_2(q)=const$ are tangent to $w_1(q), w_2(q)$ respectively. It should be noted that the theorem does not mean that the velocity vector of the coordinate curve is $w_1(q),w_2(q).$ In fact, the above theorem can be expressed in the following form. **Corollary 1.6**. *There are two direction fields $r_1, r_2,$ on the open set $U\subset S$. At a certain point $p\in U$, $r_1(p)\neq r_2(p).$ then there is a certain $p$ point neighborhood $V\subset U,$ on which there is a parameterization such that $\,\forall\,q\in V,$ The two coordinate curves passing through this point are the integral curves of $r_1 and r_2$ respectively.* The theorem can illustrate the existence of orthogonal parameterization: **Corollary 1.7**. *$\,\forall\,p\in S,$ $p$ There is a parameterization $x(u,v),$ on a certain neighborhood $V$, such that the coordinate curves $u=const, v=const$ are orthogonal to each other at any point $q\in V$. Such $x$ is called an orthogonal parameterization.* **Proof:** Take any parameterized $\bar x:\bar U\rightarrow S,$ at point $p$ Take the vector field $w_1=\bar x_{\bar u},$ $w_2=-(\bar F/\bar E)\bar x_{\bar u}+\bar x_{\bar v}.$ Then $w_1\cdot w_2=-\bar F +\bar F=0,$ is at any point $q$. Applying the theorem yields the required parameterization. Not only that, you can also arbitrarily specify two orthogonal directions at point $p$ to generate the required orthogonal parameterization. In particular, For special parameterizations, we have the following corollary: **Corollary 1.8**. *There is parameterization near the hyperbolic point, so that the coordinate curve forms an asymptote network; there is (orthogonal) parameterization near the non-umbilical point, so that the coordinate curve forms a curvature line network.* **Proof:** Near the hyperbolic point, satisfy $eg-f^2<0,$ From this, the asymptotic differential equation $e{u'}^2+2fu'v'+g{v'}^2=0$ can be linearly decomposed into the form of $(Au'+Bv')(Au'+Dv')=0$, Thus, the two direction fields are determined, and their integral curves are the asymptotes. The conclusion can be drawn from the previous inference. Near the non-umbilical point, since the differential equation of the curvature line is more complicated, from the previous inference, We might as well take the orthogonal parameterization first to make $F=0.$ Since it is a non-umbilical point, we can take the orthogonal parameterization to make $f\neq 0,$ Then from $EGf^2>0,$ the differential equation can be linearly decomposed using the same method as above to obtain the direction field, The integral curve is the curvature line. The conclusion can be drawn from the previous inference. Ruled surfaces and minimally curved surfaces ---------------- ### Ruled surface A differentiable single-parameter straight line family $\{L_t\}$, refers to the differentiable $\{\alpha(t),w(t)\},$ For each $t\in I,$ specify a point $\alpha(t)\in \mathbb{R}^2$ and vector $w(t).$ The straight line $L_t$ passes through $\alpha(t),$ with $w(t)$ as the direction. A ruled surface is a surface generated by a single-parameter straight line family, that is, $x(t,v)=\alpha(t)+vw(t),$ $(t,v)\in I\times \mathbb{R}.$ Straight line $L_t$ is called straight busbar (rulings), The curve $\alpha(t)$ is called the directrix of the surface. Generally speaking, singular points are allowed to occur in $x$. It is always assumed next that $|w(t)|=1,$ $w'(t)\neq 0.$ The latter assumption is that the surface is non-cylindrical. From the previous assumption, we have $\left<{}w(t),w'(t)\right>=0.$ We hope to find a curve $\beta(t)$ such that $\left<{}\beta'(t),w'(t)\right>=0,$ And $\beta$ falls on $x$. Let $\beta(t)=\alpha(t)+u(t)w(t),$ Then $\beta'=\alpha'+u'w+uw'.$ If the requirements are met, Then $0=\left<{}\beta',w'\right>=\left<{}\alpha',w'\right>+u\left<{}w',w'\right>,$ $u=-\frac{\left<{}\alpha',w'\right>}{\left<{}w',w'\right>}.$ Therefore, taking such $u$, the corresponding $\beta$ meets the requirements. Next, it will be explained that $\beta$ has nothing to do with the selection of the directrix. If $x(t,u)=\alpha(t)+uw(t)=\bar\alpha(t)+v(u)w(t).$ Then $\beta-\bar \beta=(\alpha-\bar\alpha)+\frac{\left<{}\bar\alpha'-\alpha',w'\right>}{\left<{}w',w'\right>}w.$ Since $\alpha-\bar\alpha=(u-v)w,$ and $\left<{}w,w'\right>=0,$ Then there is $\beta=\bar\beta.$. Therefore, such a curve $\beta$ is only related to the ruled surface itself, It is called the **waistline**, and the point on it is called the **center point** of the ruled surface. For example, the waistline of the spiral surface is the central axis, The waist line of a single-leaf hyperboloid is the latitude line with the smallest radius.\ Select $\beta$ as the directrix of the ruled surface, $x(t,u)=\beta(t)+uw(t),$ Then $x_t=\beta'+uw',$ $x_u=w,$ $x_t\wedge x_u=\beta'\wedge w+uw'\wedge w.$ $w'$ is perpendicular to $\beta',w,$ Thus we can remember $\beta'\wedge w=\lambda w',$ $\lambda=\lambda(t).$ Then $|x_t\wedge x_u|^2=(\lambda^2+u^2)|w'|^2,$ Therefore, the singular point can only appear on $u=0$, that is, the directrix $\beta$. And the singular point appears when and only if $\lambda=0,$ That is, $w(t)$ is on the tangent to the directrix. It can be expressed specifically that $\lambda=\frac{(\beta',w,w')}{|w'|^2}.$ Let's calculate the Gauss curvature of the ruled surface at the regular point. $x_{tt}=\beta''+uw'',$ $x_{tu}=w',$ $x_{uu}=0,$ thus $g=0,$ $f=\frac{(x_t,x_u,x_{tu})}{|x_t\wedge x_u|}=\frac{(\beta',w,w')}{|x_t\wedge x_u|}.$ Therefore $K=\frac{eg-f^2}{EG-F^2}=-\frac{\lambda^2 |w'|^4}{(\lambda^2+u^2)^2|w'|^4}=-\frac{\lambda^2}{(\lambda^2+u^2)^2}.$ Therefore, at the regular point, the Gauss curvature of the ruled surface $K\le 0,$ And is equal to zero only when $w(t)$ falls on the tangent line $\beta(t)$, which occurs along the straight bus. It can be seen that, If $\lambda\neq 0,$ then $|K(u)|$ is a continuous function on the straight bus, and its value is symmetric about the center point, At the center point $|K(u)|$ reaches the maximum value. The function $\lambda(t)$ is called the distribution parameter of $x$. Since the waistline has nothing to do with the selection of the directrix, The same goes for $\lambda$. If $x$ is regular, There is $N(t,u)=\frac{x_t\wedge x_u}{|x_t\wedge x_u|}=\frac{\lambda w'+uw'\wedge w}{\sqrt{\lambda^2+u^2}|w'|}.$ If $\lambda\neq 0,$ $N(t,0)=\frac{w'}{|w'|}\frac{\lambda}{|\lambda|}.$ Consider the angle between $N(t,u)$ and $N(t,0)$, $\tan\theta=\frac{u}{|\lambda|},$ Proportional to $u$, the proportional coefficient is the reciprocal of the distribution parameter. Using this property, an interesting conclusion can be drawn: The ruled surface generated by the single-parameter straight line family corresponding to the normal vector on the straight bus is exactly a hyperbolic parabola $z=kxy,$ $k=\frac{1}{\lambda}.$ For the ruled surface $x(t,v)=\alpha(t)+vw(t),$ we call it **developable**, If $(\alpha',w,w')=0.$, calculate $g=0,$ $f=\frac{(\alpha',w,w')}{|x_t\wedge x_u|}=0,$ Therefore, $K=\frac{eg-f^2}{EG-F^2}=0,$ means that the Gauss curvature of the developable surface is always zero. In fact, the reverse is also true, that is, a surface with zero Gauss curvature must also be developable. It is locally equidistant from the plane, so you can cut a piece of the surface and expand it into a plane, This is also the reason why developable surfaces are named. A basic fact is that the tangent plane of a developable curve remains unchanged along the straight generator. Just explain $\left<{}N_v,x_v\right>=\left<{}N_v,x_t\right>=0,$ that is $f=g=0.$ This has been obtained.\ Next, we explain that there are only three types of developable surfaces: cylinder, cone, and tangent surface. The part with $w'=0$ is a columnar area. After removing the part with $w'=0$, the surface is non-columnar. From the previous discussion, it can be obtained that the waistline $\beta(t),$ and the distribution parameter $\lambda=0,$ Therefore, there are singular points on the waistline. At the place where $\beta'=0$, the surface at this point is a cone; After removing the place where $\beta'=0$, we get from $\lambda=0$ that $\beta'$ and $w$ are in the same direction, That is, the surface is a tangent surface. An important property of a developable surface, or the equivalent definition is, It is the envelope surface of the single-parameter plane family. Take the regular surface $S$ and the arc length parameterized curve $\alpha,$ Assume that its direction is asymptotic. Let $N(s)=N\circ\alpha(s),$ Then $\left<{}dN(\alpha'),\alpha'\right>=\left<{}N',\alpha'\right>\neq 0,$ Thus $N'\neq 0.$ Take the ruled surface $x(s,v)=\alpha(s)+v\frac{N(s)\wedge N'(s)}{|N'(s)|}.$ Consider the family of tangent planes $\{T_{\alpha(s)}(S)\},$ of $S$ along $\alpha(s)$ Two very close planes intersect at a straight line, Take $\frac{N(s)\wedge N(s+\Delta s)}{\Delta s}$ as the direction. Let $\Delta s\rightarrow 0$ mean that the direction is $N\wedge N'.$ Therefore, the direct busbar of $x$ is the limit of the intersection of adjacent planes of the plane family. Such $x$ is called the envelope surface of the single-parameter plane family $\{T_{\alpha(s)}(S)\}$. Cylinders and cones can obviously be generated by this method. It can be calculated very directly, $(\alpha',w,w')=\frac{1}{|N'|^2}\left<{}(N\wedge N')\wedge (N\wedge N')',\alpha'\right>=\frac{1}{|N'|^2}\left<{}\left<{}N\wedge N',N''\right>N,\alpha'\right>=0,$ Therefore, the envelope surface of the single-parameter plane family is developable. For a developable surface, It is also possible to find the corresponding single-parameter plane family and use it as the envelope surface. Next, we explain that $x$ is regular near $\alpha$, and is tangent to $S$ along $\alpha$. On $\alpha$, $x_s\wedge x_v=\alpha'\wedge\frac{N\wedge N'}{|N'|}=\left<{}N',\alpha'\right>\frac{N}{|N'|}=-\frac{k_nN}{|N'|}.$ Due to the non-asymptotic direction of $\alpha'$, $k_n$ is zero everywhere, so $x$ is regular near $\alpha$, And the normal vector at $\alpha$ is consistent with $S$, that is, tangent to $S$. ### Minimal surface A regular parametric surface is **minimally small** if its mean curvature disappears everywhere. Let $x:U\subset \mathbb{R}^2\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^3$ be a regular parameterized surface. Take the bounded region $D\subset U,$ and the differentiable function $h:\bar D\rightarrow \mathbb{R}.$ $x(\bar D)$ is the **normal variation** determined by $h$, Refers to the mapping $\varphi:\bar D\times (-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^3,$ $\varphi(u,v,t)=x(u,v)+th(u,v)N(u,v).$ For each fixed $t\in (-\varepsilon,\varepsilon),$ $x^t:D\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^3,$ $x^t(u,v)=\varphi(u,v,t)$ is a parametric surface. Calculate $I^t=I-2thII+o(t).$ From the average curvature $H=\frac{1}{2}\frac{Eg-2fF+Ge}{EG-F^2},$ You can get $E^tG^t-(F^t)^2=(EG-F^2)(1-4thH)+o(t).$ Thus $x^t(\bar D)$ surface area$A(t)=\int_{\bar D}\sqrt{1-4thH+o(t)}\sqrt{EG-F^2}dudv,$ For sufficiently small $\varepsilon$, it is a differentiable function, And the derivative at zero point $A'(0)=\int_{\bar D}-2hH \sqrt{EG-F^2}dudv.$ **Proposition 1.9**. *The conditions are the same as above, $x$ is a minimal surface if and only if $A'(0)=0$ holds for any normal variation.* **Proof:** The necessity is easy to see. Assume $H(q)\neq 0,$ Take $h that is supported on the sufficiently small neighborhood of point $q$:\bar D\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ such that $h(q)=H(q),$ $hH>0.$ Then for the normal variation determined by $h$, $A'(0)<0,$ is contradictory. It should be noted that, in fact, we only stated that the minimum surface area reaches the critical value, which may not really be the minimum value. However, the definition of minimal surface was proposed by Lagrange as early as 1760, so it is still used today. When talking about minimal surfaces, soap films are often mentioned. Given a frame, put it into soapy water, Take it out carefully, and soap films will be set up on the frame. These soap films are curved surfaces with extremely small areas. In this way we create a minimal surface. Generally, the average curvature vector is defined as $H=HN,$ and let $h=H$, Then $A'(0)=\int_{\bar D}-2\left<{}H,H\right>\sqrt{EG-F^2}dudv<0,$ That is, along the average curvature vector, the area decreases near the zero point. For isothermal surfaces, that is, $E=G=\lambda^2,$ $F=0,$ there are the following propositions: **Proposition 1.10**. *Suppose $x(u,v)$ is a regular parameterized isothermal surface, then $x_{uu}+x_{vv}=2\lambda^2 H.$* **Certificate:** First from the differential, we have: $\left<{}x_{uu},x_u\right>=\left<{}x_{vu},x_v\right>=-\left<{}x_u,x_{vv}\right>,$ Thus $\left<{}x_{uu}+x_{vv},x_u\right>=0.$ Similar $\left<{}x_{uu}+x_{vv},x_v\right>=0.$ Thus $x_{uu}+x_{vv}$ is along the $N$ direction. The average curvature is calculated $H=\frac{1}{2}\frac{g+e}{\lambda^2}.$ Therefore, $2\lambda^2 H=g+e=\left<{}N,x_{uu}+x_{vv}\right>,$ Thus $x_{uu}+x_{vv}=2\lambda^2 H.$ **Corollary 1.11**. *For a regular parameterized isothermal surface $x(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v)), $ $x$ is minimal if and only if the coordinate components are all harmonic.* There are several classic examples of minimal surfaces. For example, the catenary surface is the only minimal surface among surfaces of revolution. Just take the parametric calculation of the rotation surface. This is not difficult to understand, Minimal surfaces and catenary lines are naturally formed, Therefore, there is reason to believe that the catenary surface formed by the catenary line is a minimal surface. Another example is that spiral surfaces are the only minimal surfaces among ruled surfaces, other than planes. Osserman's survey is quoted in the textbook, The property of zero-point isolation of Gaussian curvature of minimal surfaces (except planes). Except where the Gauss curvature takes zero point, since the average curvature is zero, the Gauss curvature must be negative. Since $k_1=-k_2,$ according to Euler's formula, the surface has orthogonal asymptotic curves. Obviously the straight generatrices are asymptotic curves, Since the surface is non-planar, the point $q\in W,$ can be obtained Another asymptotic curve passing through this point has $\tau=\sqrt{-K}$ as the torsion rate. Since the close plane of the asymptotic curve is the tangent plane of the surface, The straight busbar on $q$ point neighborhood $V\subset W$ is exactly the normal of another asymptotic curve family. This occurs if and only if the family of curves is a family of spirals (Bertrand curves), so $V$ is part of a spiral. Since the helical torsion is constant, it can be seen that the entire curved surface is a helical surface. Minimal surfaces are an important research topic in differential geometry, and many conclusions about it are very vivid. But it is quite difficult to prove.
