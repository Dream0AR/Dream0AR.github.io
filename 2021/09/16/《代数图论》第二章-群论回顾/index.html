permutation group ------ Recall that any subgroup of a symmetry group is called a permutation group, The automorphism group of a graph is a permutation group. A permutation representation of the group $G$ representation)** refers to the homomorphism of $G$ to a certain symmetry group $\operatorname{Sym}(V)$, It is also called the **action (action)** of the group $G$ on the set $V$. A representation is faithful if it is injective, mapping identity elements only to identity permutations. The effect of group $G$ on set $V$ also induces other effects, As it does on the set $V$ power set $2^V$, it gives the permutation between subsets. Note that the effect preserves the size of the subset, so it can also be applied to all $k-$ subsets of $V$, Or change the subset to an ordered tuple. A subset $S$ of a set $V$ is said to be $G-$**invariant** if $\,\forall\,s\in S, g\in G,$ $s^g\in S.$ At this time, any $g\in G$ gives the replacement of the elements in $S$, It can be **restricted** to $S$, denoted as $g|_S.$, and the restriction of the entire substitution is denoted as $G|_S,$ or $G^S.$ The effect of $G$ on $V$ is **transitive**, if $x,y\in V,$ is given arbitrarily There exists $g\in G$ such that $x^g=y.$ The invariant subset $S$ of $G-$ is called an orbit if $G|_S$ is transportable on $S$. $\,\forall\,x\in V,$ $x^G:=\{x^g:g\in G\}$ is an orbit. The orbitals do not intersect each other, giving a partition of $V$. Each element falls in exactly one orbital. Each $G-$ invariant subset is the union of a series of orbitals, so the orbits can also be called minimal $G-$ invariant subsets. count ---- Let $G$ be the permutation group of $V$, $\,\forall\,x\in V,$ **Stabilizer**$G_x$ is the total permutation such that $x^g=x$, forming a subgroup. Given $S=\{x_1,\cdots,x_r\}\subset V,$ **Pointwise Stabilizer**$G_{x_1,\cdots,x_r}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^r G_{x_i},$ **Set Stabilizer**$G_S$ is a global permutation that only requires $S^g=S$. It is easy to see that the former is a subgroup of the latter. **Lemma 1.1**. *Suppose $G$ acts on $V$, and $S$ is the orbit of $G$. If $x,y\in S,$ then all the permutations from $x$ to $y$ will be mapped to a certain right coset of $G_x$. In turn, all the permutations in the right coset of $G_x$ will map $x$ to the same point in $S$.* The proofs in this section are basically basic exercises in abstract algebra and will be skipped here. **Lemma 1.2** (Orbital Stable Set Theorem). *$|G_x||x^G|=|G|.$* Take $h\in G,$ and say that an element of the form $g^{-1}hg$ is conjugate to $h$**, The entire set of elements that are conjugated to $h$ is called the **conjugate class** of $h$. The mapping $\tau_g:h\rightarrow g^{-1}hg$ gives the permutation of $G$, Therefore $G$ has a conjugation effect on itself, with the conjugate class as the orbit. If $H$ is a subgroup of $G$, Then $g^{-1}Hg$ is also a subgroup, which is said to be conjugate with $H$. **Lemma 1.3**. *Suppose $G$ has an effect on $V$, then $\,\forall\,x\in V, g\in G,$ $g^{-1}G_x g=G_{x^g}.$* Let $\operatorname{fix}(g)$ represent the number of fixed points under permutation $g$. We are familiar with the following lemma: **Lemma 1.4** (Burnside). *The number of orbits $G$ acts on $V$ is equal to the average number of fixed points of elements in $G$.* asymmetric graph -------- A graph is asymmetric if its automorphism group is trivial. In this section we prove that almost all graphs are asymmetric, that is, for graphs with $n$ points, The proportion of asymmetric graphs approaches $1.$ when $n\rightarrow \infty$ Suppose $V$ contains $n$ points, let $K_V$ be the complete graph of $n$ points. All graphs containing $n$ points are obtained by $K_V$ by deleting edges, so there are a total of $2^{\binom{n}{2} }$ graphs, One-to-one correspondence with $E(K_V)$ subset. Given a graph $ It can be seen that the isomorphism class is the orbit of the symmetry group $\operatorname{Sym}(V)$ acting on the power set $E(K_V)$. According to the orbital stable set theorem, there is the following lemma: **Lemma 1.5**. *The size of the isomorphic class containing $X$ is $\frac{n!}{|\operatorname{Aut}(X)|}.$* By Burnside's lemma, we count isomorphic classes. Set $g$ (the generated subgroup) to act on $E(K_V)$ and there are $r$ orbitals, It is recorded as $\operatorname{orb}_2(g)=r.$ Then when $g$ acts on the power set of $E(K_V)$, These orbits are all minimal $g-$ fixed points, and combined there are a total of $2^r$ fixed points. Therefore, the number of isomorphism classes of the graph is $\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{g\in \operatorname{Sym}(V)}2^{\operatorname{orb}_2(g)}$. If all graphs are asymmetric, then each equivalence class contains $n!$ graphs, Therefore, there are $\frac{2^{\binom{n}{2} } }{n!}$ equivalence classes. Next we show that in fact when $n\rightarrow \infty$, the number of equivalence classes tends to this value, It is further inferred that almost all graphs are asymmetric. **Lemma 1.6**. *The number of isomorphism classes of a graph with $n$ vertices is at most $(1+o(1))\frac{2^{\binom{n}{2} } }{n!}$.* **Proof:** It is said that all the points that cannot be fixed by a certain displacement are the **support** of the displacement, That is, the effect of substitution is only reflected on the support. It is asserted that for all substitutions with support size $2r$, The maximum value of $\operatorname{orb}_2$ is achieved by a permutation consisting of $r$ swaps. The non-permuted fixed point of edge $xy$ is if and only if $x and y$ are both in the support and not in the same permutation, Or one is in the support and the other is not. The set exchange contains $k$ exchanges, Then there are $\binom{n-2r}{2}+k$ immovable edges, and each edge is a separate orbit. For the moving edge, directly consider the number of orbits: one is in the support, The number of orbitals contributed by the edge where the other is not present is at most $(n-2r)(k+2(r-k)/3)=(n-2r)(2r+k)/3$; The number of orbits that are all in the support and not in the same swap is at most $2k\cdot 2(r-k)/3+k(k-1)+\binom{2(r-k)}{2}/3=4/3 \cdot k(r-k)+k(k-1)+(r-k)(2r-2k-1)/3=[2r^2+k^2-r-2k]/3.$ The total of the previous ones is at most $\binom{n-2r}{2}+[r(2n-2r-1)+k(k+n-2r+1)]/3$, Reaching the maximum value at $k=r$$(n-2r)(n-2r-1)/2+nr-r^2=n^2/2-rn+r^2-n/2+r=\binom{n}{2}+r(r-n+1).$ And for a permutation consisting of $k=r$ substitutions, The previous number of orbits only performs a scaled estimate on the $r-k$ part (the non-swapping part), so when $k=r$, The calculation is precise, Therefore, the maximum value of $\operatorname{orb}_2$ is indeed realized by a permutation consisting of $r$ swaps, And the specific value has been given. Note that the maximum estimate of the orbital number also holds for permutations with non-even support sizes, That is, $2r$ is replaced by the support size $t\in[2,n]$ (the maximum estimate may not be realized at this time). Then the maximum estimate of the orbital number $\varphi(t)=\binom{n}{2}+\frac{t}{2}(\frac{t}{2}-n+1)$ is a quadratic function, Take $t=n-1$ as the axis of symmetry. Take $m\le n-2,$ and now divide $\operatorname{Sym}(V)$ into three categories: identity transformation, Supports permutations and remaining permutations whose size does not exceed $m$. Their sizes do not exceed $1,\binom{n}{m}m! Except for the case where $S$ is a single point and a universal set, all other non-primitive systems are called non-trivial. A group is said to be primitive if it has no non-trivial and non-primitive systems. Otherwise it is non-primitive. **Lemma 1.10**. *$G$ can be transferred to $V$, $x\in V.$ $G$ is a primitive if and only if $G_x$ is a maximal subgroup of $G$.* **Proof:** Let us prove that $G$ has a non-trivial non-primitive system if and only if $G_x$ is not a maximal subgroup of $G$. Suppose $G$ has a non-trivial non-primitive system, let $x\in B,$ $B$ be a non-primitive block. Prove below that $G_x\subsetneq G_B\subsetneq G.$ Since $\,\forall\,g\in G_x,$ $x\in B\cap B^g,$ Therefore $B=B^g,$ $g\in G_B.$ Take another element in $B$ $y\neq x.$ Due to portability, $h\in G$ exists such that $y=x^h.$ and thus $y\in B\cap B^h,$ $B=B^h,$ $h\in G_B.$ However, $h\notin G_x,$ therefore $G_x$ is really included in $G_B.$ Let $G_x\subsetneq H\subsetneq G,$ We explain that the orbit of $H$ is the non-trivial non-primitive system of $G$. Let $B$ be the orbit of $H$ containing $x$, Taking $g\in G,$ requires proving that either $B=B^g,$ or $B\cap B^g=\varnothing.$ Suppose $y\in B\cap B^g,$ Since $x,y$ are both in orbit $B$, $\,\exists\,h\in H,$ Make $y=x^h.$ and since $y\in B^g,$ has elements $x^{h'},$ $x^{h'g}=y.$ in $B$ Therefore $x^{h'gh^{-1} }=x,$ $h'gh^{-1}\in G_x\subsetneq H,$ and thus $g\in H.$ Since $B$ is the orbit of $H$, there is $B=B^g.$ Since $G_x\subsetneq H,$ $B$ is not only composed of $x$. In order to illustrate that the orbit of $H$ is not the entire $V,$ Recall that $\,\forall\,y\in V,$ $G$ replaces all mappings from $x$ to $y$ to a certain right coset of $G_x$. If $H$ is also transferable, then $G_x H\supset G.$ However, $G_x H=H$, therefore $H=G,$ It is inconsistent with $H$ being a real subgroup. Therefore $B\neq V$, that is, $B$ is a non-trivial non-primitive system. Originality and connectivity --------------- Another description of originality is reflected in the orbit of $G$ acting on $V\times V$. Let's do some preparatory work first. A path of a directed graph refers to a sequence composed of different vertices $\{u_0,\cdots,u_r\},$ $(u_{i-1},u_i)$ is an arc; the weak path of a directed graph (weak path)** only requires $(u_{i-1},u_i)$ or $(u_{i},u_{i-1})$ to be an arc. A directed graph is **strongly connected**, if any two points are connected by a path; Calling it **weakly connected** means that any two points are connected by a weak path. The weak connectivity of a directed graph is the connectivity of an undirected graph regardless of direction. The **strongly connected branch** of a directed graph, Refers to a strongly connected maximal induced subgraph. According to the definition, a single point is strongly connected, Therefore a strongly connected branch constitutes a partition of the graph. The **in-valency** of a vertex in a directed graph is the number of arcs ending at that point; **Out-valency** is the number of arcs starting from this point. **Lemma 1.11**. *If $D$ is a directed graph with the same out-degree and in-degree of all points, then $D$ is strongly connected if and only if it is weakly connected.* **Proof:** Just prove that when $D$ is weakly connected, it is also strongly connected. Otherwise, $D$ can be split into strongly connected branches $D_1,\cdots,D_r.$ If there is an arc starting from $D_i$ to $D_j,$ Then all arcs connecting $D_i, D_j$ must start from $D_i$, Otherwise $D_i,D_j$ can be merged into strongly connected subgraphs. Construct a directed graph $D',$ with the strongly connected branches of $D$ as vertices. There is a directed edge connecting $D_i$ to $D_j$ if and only if there is an arc in $D$ starting from $D_i$ to $D_j.$ Since $D$ is weakly connected, so is $D'$. Cycles cannot appear in $D'$, Otherwise, the union of all strongly connected branches involved in the ring is strongly connected. Therefore, it is easy to prove that at least one point in $D'$ has an in-degree of zero, and we might as well assume that it corresponds to a strongly connected branch $D_1.$ For all arcs involving $D_1$, if its end point is within $D_1$, the starting point must be within $D_1$, Out-degree and in-degree cancel; but there is at least one edge starting from $D_1$ and ending outside $D_1$, Because $D'$ is weakly connected. Therefore, the sum of the out-degrees of all points in $D_1$ must be greater than the in-degree, which is inconsistent with the condition. Recall that when $G$ can be transferred to $V$, the orbit $\Omega$ on $V\times V$ can be regarded as a directed graph. When $\Omega$ is asymmetric, the graph is a directed graph. Since $G$ is transferable, all points have the same out-degree. The in-degrees are the same. And since the sum of out-degrees is equal to the sum of in-degrees (equal to the number of arcs), the out-degrees of all points are equal to the in-degrees. Therefore, the strong connectivity of the directed graph corresponding to the orbit is equal to the weak connectivity, which is generally called the connectivity of the orbit. **Lemma 1.12**. *$G$ is transferable on $V$. $G$ is primitive if and only if every non-diagonal orbit is connected.* **Proof:** Suppose $G$ is non-primitive, $B_1,\cdots, B_r$ are non-primitive systems. Let $x\neq y\in B_1,$ $\Omega$ be the orbit containing $(x,y)$ on $V\times V$. For $g\in G,$ $x^g,y^g$ are in the same non-original block. Therefore, the starting and end points of each arc of $\Omega$ are in the same block, Obviously not connected. On the contrary, suppose a certain orbit of $\Omega$ is not connected, and $B$ is all the vertices of a certain connected branch of $\Omega$. At this time $\,\forall\,g\in G,$ $B^g$ is obviously connected, Therefore $B^g=B$ or $B^g\cap B=\varnothing.$ Therefore $B$ is a non-original block, $G$ is non-primitive.
