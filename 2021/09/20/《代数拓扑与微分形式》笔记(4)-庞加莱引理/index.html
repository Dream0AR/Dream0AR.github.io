de Rham’s cohomological Poincaré lemma -------------------------- In this section we hope to prove, $H^\ast (\mathbb{R}^n\times \mathbb{R}^1)\cong H^\ast (\mathbb{R}^n)$. We record $\pi:\mathbb{R}^n\times \mathbb{R}^1\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ as projection, $s:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n\times \mathbb{R}^1$ is zero section, That is, $\pi(x,t)=x$, $s(x)=(x,0)$, $x\in \mathbb{R}^n$. We have $\pi\circ s=\mathrm{id}\Rightarrow s^\ast \circ\pi^\ast =\mathrm{id}.$ But $s\circ\pi \neq \mathrm{id}$, But there is indeed $\pi^\ast \circ s^\ast =\mathrm{id}$. In order to prove this point, $D$ is introduced similar to algebraic topology, We introduce the **homopy operator**$K:\Omega^\ast (\mathbb{R}^n\times \mathbb{R}^1)\rightarrow \Omega^{\ast -1}(\mathbb{R}^n\times \mathbb{R}^1).$ If $\mathrm{id}-\pi^\ast \circ s^\ast =\pm (dK \pm Kd)$, Then $K$ is called a homotopy operator, Remember $\pi^\ast \circ s^\ast \simeq \mathrm{id}$**chain homotopy**. When the two mappings of chain homotopy act on the element $\omega$ in the cohomology group, because $d\omega=0$, Only the $\pm dK\omega$ term is left, which is the proper form. Therefore, the two mappings are the same in the homology sense, Thus $\pi^\ast \circ s^\ast =id$. Linearly split the elements in $\Omega^\ast (\mathbb{R}^n\times \mathbb{R}^1)$ into $\pi^\ast \phi\cdot f(x,t)$ (no $dt$ items), And $\pi^\ast \phi\wedge f(x,t)dt$ (including $dt$ term). $\phi\in \Omega^\ast (\mathbb{R}^n)$. Now let $K:\pi^\ast \phi\cdot f(x,t)\mapsto 0, \pi^\ast \phi\wedge f(x,t)dt\mapsto \pi^\ast \phi\cdot \int_0^tf(x,u)du$. **Lemma 1.1**. *$K$ is the homotopy operator.* Calculate the value of $(\mathrm{id}-\pi^\ast s^\ast )$ and $dK-Kd$ acting on $\omega$, Consider the above two situations respectively based on linearity, Just get $\mathrm{id}-\pi^\ast s^\ast =(-1)^{q-1}(dK-Kd)$. The formula is relatively long but the calculation is relatively simple, so you need to pay attention to the symbols. Thus, we have: **Proposition 1.2**. *$\pi^\ast$, $s^\ast$ are isomorphic to each other, $H^\ast (\mathbb{R}^n\times \mathbb{R}^1)\cong H^\ast (\mathbb{R}^n)$.* and corollary: **Corollary 1.3**. *$H^q(\mathbb{R}^n)=H^q(\ast )= \begin{cases} \mathbb{R}, \quad &q=0\\ 0, &q\neq 0 \end{cases}$, Here $\ast =\mathbb{R}^0$, which is a single point space.* More generally, we have the following theorem: **Theorem 1.4** (de Rham cohomology and homotopy axiom). *$M,N$ are smooth manifolds, $f,g$ are two smooth homotopy smooth mappings from $M$ to $N$, then $f^\ast =g^\ast$.* **Proof** Let $F:M\times I\rightarrow N$ be smooth homotopy, $\,\forall\,\omega\in \Omega^q(N)$, $F^\ast \omega\in \Omega^q(M\times I)$. Locally decompose $F^\ast \omega$ into $F^\ast \omega=\omega_1+\omega_2\wedge dt$, $\omega_1,\omega_2$ are in the form of $q, q-1$ respectively. Following the previous construction method, $K$ is constructed to map $\omega$ to $0$, Integrate the function terms in $\omega_2$ with respect to $t$. Then use $F(x,0)=f(x)$, $F(x,1)=g(x)$, just use the simple Leibnitz rule to prove the following formula: $$dK(F^\ast \omega)\pm Kd(F^\ast \omega)=g^\ast \omega-f^\ast \omega.$$ Note that $dF^\ast =F^\ast d$, so the above formula is true, which means that $KF^\ast$ is a homotopy operator, $g^\ast \simeq f^\ast$, are equal in the sense of a homology group. **Corollary 1.5**. *Two manifolds that are homotopically equivalent have the same de Rham cohomology.* In particular, there are: **Corollary 1.6**. *If $A$ is the deformation contraction of $M$, then $A$ and $M$ have the same de Rham cohomology.* Poincaré's Lemma for Compact Cohomology ----------------------- Similarly, for tight cohomology, we wish to prove: $H^\ast _c(\mathbb{R}^n\times \mathbb{R}^1)\cong H^{\ast -1}_c(\mathbb{R}^n).$ At this time $\pi^\ast$ can no longer maintain a compact support set, and we need to consider a new mapping $\pi_\ast$. $\,\forall\,\omega\in \Omega^\ast _c(M\times \mathbb{R}^1)$, Divide it into two forms of linear combinations according to whether it contains $dt$ terms, That is, $\pi^\ast \phi\cdot f(x,t)$ and $\pi^\ast \phi\wedge f(x,t)dt$, Among them $\phi\in \Omega^\ast (M)$, $f(x,t)\in C_c^\infty (M\times \mathbb{R}^1)$. We define $\pi_\ast :\phi\cdot f(x,t)\mapsto 0$, $\phi\wedge f(x,t)dt\mapsto \phi\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^1}f(x,t)dt$. After simple verification, it was found that $d\pi_\ast =\pi_\ast d$ is a chain mapping, Thus $\pi_\ast can be induced: H^q_c(M\times \mathbb{R}^1)\rightarrow H^{q-1}_c(M)$. We next find its inverse mapping $e_\ast$. Definition $e_\ast :\Omega^q_c(M)\rightarrow \Omega_c^{q+1}(M\times \mathbb{R}^1)$, $e_\ast (\phi)=\pi^\ast \phi \wedge e$, Where $e=e(t)dt\in \Omega_c^1(\mathbb{R}^1)$, $\int_{\mathbb{R}^1}e(t)dt=1$ (such as bump 1-form). It is easy to verify that it is also a chain mapping, Induces mappings on tight cohomology groups. What follows is the following proposition: **Proposition 1.7**. *$\pi_\ast \circ e_\ast =\mathrm{id}$, $e_\ast \circ \pi_\ast \simeq \mathrm{id}$* **Proof** In the first part, it is obvious that the main need is to construct the homotopy operator $K$ to connect $e_\ast$ and $\pi_\ast$. Definition $K:\pi^\ast \phi\cdot f(x,t)\mapsto 0$, $\pi^\ast \phi\wedge f(x,t)dt\mapsto \pi^\ast \phi\cdot \int_{-\infty}^{t}f(x,u)du-\pi^\ast \phi\cdot A(t)\cdot \int_{\mathbb{R}^1}f(x, t)dt$, Where $A(t)=\int_{-\infty}^t e(u)du$. Just verify $id-e_\ast \circ \pi_\ast =(-1)^{q-1}(dK-Kd)$, pay attention to the use of compactness, A lot of points accumulate to $0$. This leads to the following proposition: **Proposition 1.8**. *$\pi_\ast ,e_\ast$ are inverse mappings to each other, $H_c^\ast (M\times \mathbb{R}^1)\cong H_c^{\ast -1}(M)$* and corollary: **Corollary 1.9**. *$H^q(\mathbb{R}^n)=\begin{cases} \mathbb{R}, \quad &q=n\\ 0, &q\neq n \end{cases}$.* Through the $0$-form generator $1$, You can use $e_\ast$ to find the $n$-form generator $e(x_1)\cdots e(x_n){} dx_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{} dx_{ {}n}$, It is a bump $n$-form on $\mathbb{R}^n$, and the compact support set can be arbitrarily small. degree of inverse compact mapping ---------- Next is the application of Poincaré's lemma on compact supports, which gives smooth invariants of inverse compact maps. If $f:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ is smooth and inversely compact, Then there is pull back $f^\ast :\Omega^\ast _c(\mathbb{R}^n)\rightarrow \Omega^\ast _c(\mathbb{R}^n)$, Inducing $f^\ast :H^\ast _c(\mathbb{R}^n)\rightarrow H^\ast _c(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is defined. Take $[\alpha]\in H^n_c(\mathbb{R}^n)$ as the generator, $\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\alpha =1$, $f^\ast [\alpha]=c[\alpha]$, $c$ is called the degree of $f$, denoted as $\deg f$. It is easy to see $\deg f=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f^\ast \alpha$. Surprisingly, $\deg f\in \mathbb{Z}$. ### Proof First, give some definitions in differential geometry: If $f:\mathbb{R}^m\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ is a smooth mapping, Then it induces $f_{\ast p}:T_p\mathbb{R}^m\rightarrow T_{f(p)}\mathbb{R}^n$ to be a tangent mapping. If it is not full, $p$ is called the critical point and $f(p)$ is the critical value. The remaining points in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are regular values. **Proposition 1.10**. *If $f:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ is not full, then $\deg f=0$.* **Proof** $f$ is inversely compact, so $f(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is closed, $\mathbb{R}^n-f(\mathbb{R}^n)$Open. There is an open set $U\subset \mathbb{R}^n-f(\mathbb{R}^n)$. Let the generator $[\alpha]$ support be in $U$, then $f^\ast \alpha=0$, $\deg f=0$. Then first admit a theorem: **Theorem 1.11** (Sard's theorem). *For any positive integer $m,n$, the critical value set is the zero test set of $\mathbb{R}^n$.* Next, prove the main proposition: $\deg f\in \mathbb{Z}$. **Proof** Suppose $f:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ is full, according to Sard's theorem, Almost everywhere in $f(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is a regular value. Let $q\in f(\mathbb{R}^n)$ be a regular value. $\,\forall\,p\in f^{-1}(q)$, $f_{\ast p}$ is a full linear mapping, thus isomorphism. According to the inverse function theorem, $f$ is diffeomorphic near point $p$. So $f^{-1}(q)$ is a discrete point set. And $f$ is inversely compact, and $q$ points are inversely compact, so $f^{-1}(q)$ is a finite point set, Denoted as $f^{-1}(q)=\{p_1,...,p_N\}$. For each $p_i$, take $U_i\approx f(U_i)$ such that $U_i\cap U_j=\varnothing$. Take the support $\alpha \in \Omega_c^n(\mathbb{R}^n)$ in $\bigcap_i f(U_i)$. Thus $\operatorname{supp}f^\ast \alpha\subset \bigcup U_i$. $\int_{U_i}f^\ast \alpha=\pm \int_{f(U_i)} \alpha=\pm 1$, The sign is determined by the diffeomorphism Jacobi sign. So $\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f^\ast \alpha=\sum_{f^{-1}(q)}\pm 1\in \mathbb{Z}$. The article was last updated on 2021-09-20 16:39:03
