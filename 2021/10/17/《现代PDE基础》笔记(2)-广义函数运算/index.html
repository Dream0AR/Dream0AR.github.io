limit ---- For the generalized function sequence $\{T_k\},$ of a certain basic function space If for any element $\varphi,$ on its basic function space If $\left<{}T_k,\varphi\right>\rightarrow 0,$, it is said that $T_k$ (weakly) converges to $0.$ If $T_k-T$ converges to $0,$, then the limit of $T_k$ is said to be $T,$ and is denoted as $T_k\rightarrow T.$ For normal functions, we can do regularization $J_\varepsilon.$ Next we explain, There are similar regularizations for generalized functions. **Theorem 1.1**. *If $T\in \mathscr{D}'(\mathbb{R}_y^n),$ $T_\varepsilon(x):=\left<{}T_y,\alpha_\varepsilon(x-y)\right>\in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}_x^n),$ $\alpha_\varepsilon$ is shown in the previous section.* **Proof:** It is not difficult to explain that when $x\rightarrow x_0$, $\alpha_\varepsilon(x-y)\xrightarrow{\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}_y^n)} \alpha_\varepsilon(x_0-y)$(equal degree continuous), Therefore $\left<{}T,\alpha_\varepsilon(x-y)\right>\rightarrow\left<{}T,\alpha_\varepsilon(x_0-y)\right>,$ $T_\varepsilon(x)$ is a continuous function. Similarly, $\frac{\alpha_\varepsilon(x+\Delta x_k,y)-\alpha_\varepsilon(x,y)}{\Delta x_k}\xrightarrow{\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}_y^n)} \partial_k \alpha_\varepsilon(x,y),$ Thereby there is $$\frac{T_\varepsilon(x+\Delta x_k)-T_\varepsilon(x)}{\Delta x_k}=\left<{}T,\frac{\alpha_\varepsilon(x+\Delta x_k,y)-\alpha_\varepsilon(x,y)}{\Delta x_k}\right>\rightarrow\left<{}T,\partial_k \alpha_\varepsilon(x,y)\right>,$$ Therefore $T_\varepsilon(x)$ is differentiable with respect to $x_k$. In the same way, it can be proved that it has any order of derivatives, Therefore it is a smooth function. When $T$ is a normal function, the functional is the integral, Therefore, regularization of generalized functions is a generalization of regularization of normal functions. Of course, the following theorem makes regularization meaningful: **Theorem 1.2**. *$T\in \mathscr{D}'(\mathbb{R}^n)$, $T_\varepsilon\xrightarrow{\mathscr{D}'} T.$* **Certificate:** That is to say, $\left<{}T_\varepsilon,\varphi\right>=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} T_\varepsilon\varphi dx\rightarrow \left<{}T,\varphi\right>,$ $\,\forall\,\varphi\in \mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^n).$ Since $\varphi$ has compact support, the Riemann integral can be used to approximate the integral in the formula. However, after writing the integral in summation form, $\alpha_\varepsilon(x-y)$ can be combined with $\varphi(x)$ in advance, that is $$\sum_i\alpha_\varepsilon(x_i-y)\varphi(x_i)\Delta x_i\xrightarrow{\mathscr{E} }\int_{\mathbb{R}_x^n}\alpha_\varepsilon(x-y)\varphi(x)dx,$$ Thus $\left<{}T_\varepsilon,\varphi\right>=\left<{}T,\varphi_\varepsilon\right>\rightarrow \left<{}T,\varphi\right>,$ The proposition is proved. **Theorem 1.3**. *$T\in \mathscr{E}'(\mathbb{R}^n)$, $T_\varepsilon\xrightarrow{\mathscr{E}'} T.$* **Certificate:** That is to say, $\left<{}T_\varepsilon,\varphi\right>=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} T_\varepsilon\varphi dx\rightarrow \left<{}T,\varphi\right>,$ $\,\forall\,\varphi\in \mathscr{E}(\mathbb{R}^n).$ Since $T$ has compact support, It is not difficult to prove that $T_\varepsilon$ also has compact support and is sufficiently close to $\operatorname{supp}T.$ The $\zeta$ of the compact support is always $1 in a certain neighborhood of $\operatorname{supp}T$. From the previous theorem, $\left<{}T_\varepsilon,\zeta\varphi\right>\rightarrow \left<{}T,\zeta\varphi\right>.$ Since $\zeta\varphi=\varphi,$ in the neighborhood of $\operatorname{supp}T$ And for sufficiently small $\varepsilon,$, the neighborhood contains the supports of $T,T_\varepsilon$, so $$\left<{}T_\varepsilon,\zeta\varphi\right>=\left<{}T_\varepsilon,\varphi\right>\rightarrow \left<{}T,\varphi\right>=\left<{}T,\zeta\varphi\right>,$$ The proposition is proved. Derivative ---- Let $T\in\mathscr{D}'(\mathbb{R}^n),$ Define the **(generalized) derivative of $T$ with respect to $x_k$**$\frac{\partial {}T}{\partial {}x_k}\in \mathscr{D}'(\mathbb{R}^n),$ satisfy $$\left<{}\frac{\partial {}T}{\partial {}x_k},\varphi\right>=-\left<{}T,\frac{\partial {}\varphi}{\partial {}x_k}\right>.$$ According to the integral formula by parts, when $T$ is a $C^1$ function, the generalized derivative is the derivative in the general sense. Therefore, the concept of generalized derivative is an extension of the concept of ordinary derivative. In particular, for some normal functions, It cannot be differentiated according to the usual concepts. But consider it as a generalized function, You can get the generalized derivatives in the generalized function space, which is often very useful. Define higher order derivatives similarly $$\left<{}\partial^\alpha T,\varphi\right>=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\left<{}T,\partial^\alpha \varphi\right>.$$ It can be seen that derivatives of any order of generalized functions exist, and mixed partial derivatives have nothing to do with order. An interesting example is, if $\operatorname{supp}T=\{0\},$ Then $T$ can only be a finite linear combination of $\delta$ and its derivatives. The proof is given by performing Taylor expansion on $\varphi$. multiplier ---- For $T\in \mathscr{D}'(\mathbb{R}^n),$ $\alpha\in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n),$ Define $\alpha T\in \mathscr{D}'(\mathbb{R}^n)$ as $$\left<{}\alpha T,\varphi\right>=\left<{}T,\alpha\varphi\right>.$$ Call $\alpha$ the **multiplier** of $\mathscr{D}'(\mathbb{R}^n)$. Similarly it is also a multiplier of $\mathscr{E}'(\mathbb{R}^n)$, But it is generally not necessarily a multiplier of $\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^n)$. Similar to normal functions, we have the Leibniz formula: $$\partial^r(\alpha T)=\sum_{p+q=r} \frac{r!}{p!q!}(\partial^p \alpha \cdot \partial^q T).$$ independent variable transformation ---------- Modeling the argument transformation of the $C_c^\infty$ function, for $T\in \mathscr{D}'(\Omega_x),$ Give smooth homeomorphism $\psi:\Omega_x\rightarrow \Omega_y,$ Define $S\in \mathscr{D}'(O_y):$ $$\left<{}S,\varphi\right>=\left<{}T,\det|\psi'|\varphi\circ\psi\right>.$$ When $T$ is a normal function, $S(y)=T(\psi^{-1}(y)).$ This definition is naturally an extension of the usual definition. Convolution ---- For $f,g\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n),$ consider the role of $f\ast g$ on $\varphi$, Analogously define the convolution between generalized integrals. Let $S, T$ be generalized functions, define **convolution**$S\ast T:$ $$\left<{}S\ast T,\varphi\right>=\left<{}S_x,\left<{}T_y,\varphi(x+y)\right>\right>,$$ $\,\forall\,\varphi\in \mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^n).$ is the same as the normal function, Convolution of generalized functions doesn't always make sense either. When at least one of $S,T\in \mathscr{D}'(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is in $\mathscr{E}'(\mathbb{R}^n)$, Convolution is meaningful, $S\ast T\in \mathscr{D}'(\mathbb{R}^n).$ The proof process is divided into two parts. First, only when $T\in \mathscr{D}'/\mathscr{E}'$, $\psi:=\left<{}T_y,\varphi(x+y)\right>\in \mathscr{E}/\mathscr{D},$ Only then can the definition be meaningful; secondly, when $\varphi_\nu\xrightarrow{\mathscr{D} } 0$, There are $\psi_\nu\xrightarrow{\mathscr{E}/\mathscr{D} }0,$ Therefore, the linear functional is continuous. The proof method is similar to the previous one and is omitted here. It can be proved that For $R,S,T\in \mathscr{D}'(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and at least two of them have compact supports, The convolution of generalized functions (same as normal convolution) satisfies the following properties: 1. $\operatorname{supp}(S\ast T)\subset \operatorname{supp}(S)+\operatorname{supp}(T);$ 2. $(R\ast S)\ast T=R\ast (S\ast T);$ 3. $S\ast T=T\ast S;$ 4. $\delta\ast T=T\ast \delta=T;$ 5. When $\alpha_1+\alpha_2=\alpha$, $\partial^\alpha(S\ast T)=(\partial^{\alpha_1})\ast (\partial^{\alpha_2});$ 6. $T\in \mathscr{D}'/\mathscr{E}',$ When $\varphi\in \mathscr{D}/\mathscr{E}$, $T\ast \varphi=\left<{}T_y,\varphi(x-y)\right>\in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}_x^n).$ The first four properties describe $\mathscr{E}'(\mathbb{R}^n)$ regarding the usual linear operations and convolution operations forming a commutative algebra with unit element $\delta$, It is called **convolution algebra**.
