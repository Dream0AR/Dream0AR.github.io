This lesson will generalize the MV sequence from two open sets to countably multiple open sets. The main idea comes from Weil. Promotion of countable multiple open sets ------------------ $\{U_\alpha\}$ is an open covering for $M$, and $I$ is a countably ordered set. There are the following inclusive sequences: $$M\leftarrow \bigsqcup_{\alpha_0} U_{\alpha_0}\begin{matrix} \xleftarrow{\partial_0}\\\xleftarrow{\partial_1} \end{matrix} \bigsqcup_{\alpha_0<\alpha_1} U_{\alpha_0\alpha_1}\begin{matrix} \xleftarrow{\partial_0}\\\xleftarrow{\partial_1}\\\xleftarrow{\partial_2} \end{matrix}\cdots$$ Here $\partial_i$ means ignoring the $i$th open set. Then such an inclusion map $\partial_i$ induces a restriction map $\delta_i$. Then there are the following restriction sequences: $$\Omega^*(M)\rightarrow \prod_{\alpha_0} \Omega^*(U_{\alpha_0})\begin{matrix} \xrightarrow{\delta_0}\\\xrightarrow{\delta_1} \end{matrix} \prod_{\alpha_0<\alpha_1} \Omega^*(U_{\alpha_0\alpha_1})\begin{matrix} \xrightarrow{\delta_0}\\\xrightarrow{\delta_1}\\\xrightarrow{\delta_2} \end{matrix}\cdots$$ Define the difference operator $$\delta: \prod_{\alpha_0<\cdots<\alpha_p} \Omega^\ast (U_{\alpha_0\cdots\alpha_p})\rightarrow \prod_{\alpha_0<\cdots<\alpha_{p+1} } \Omega^\ast (U_{\alpha_0\cdots\alpha_{p+1} }),$$ $\delta=\sum_{i=0}^{p+1}(-1)^i\delta_i$. The component of $\delta\omega$ on $\Omega^\ast (U_{\alpha_0\cdots\alpha_{p+1} })$ is $\sum_{i=0}^{p+1}(-i)^i \omega_{\alpha_0,...,\hat\alpha_i,...,\alpha_{p+1} }$. It is easy to prove that $\delta^2=0$. In fact, So far, the subscript of $\omega_{\alpha_0...\alpha_p}$ requires $\alpha_0<...<\alpha_p$, But you can add a definition to remove this requirement. For any $\omega_{\alpha_0...\alpha_p}$, Use $\tau$ to replace the subscripts, Define it as $\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\omega_{\alpha_\tau}$. It is easy to verify that this does not conflict with the previous system. **Proposition 1.1** (Generalized MV sequence). *We have the exact sequence: $0\rightarrow \Omega^\ast (M)\xrightarrow{r}\prod \Omega^\ast (U_{\alpha_0})\xrightarrow{\delta}\prod\Omega^\ast (U_{\alpha_0\alpha_1})\rightarrow\cdots$* **Proof:** Obviously the restriction map $r$ is injective, It only needs to be proved that it is exact at $\prod \Omega^\ast (U_{\alpha_0...\alpha_p})$. $\,\forall\,\alpha_0,\alpha_1$, $(\delta_\omega)_{\alpha_0\alpha_1}=\omega_{\alpha_1}-\omega_{\alpha_0}=0$ if and only if $\omega_{\alpha_1}$, $\omega_{\alpha_0}$ is compatible on $U_{\alpha_0\alpha_1}$, If and only if $\omega_{\alpha}$ can be defined as $\eta$ as a whole, thus $r(\eta)=\omega$. For the following complex shape, since $\delta^2=0$, Just verify $\ker\delta\subset \operatorname{Im}\delta$. Take $\tau_{\alpha_0\cdots\alpha_{p-1} }=\sum_{\alpha}\rho_\alpha\omega_{\alpha\alpha_0\cdots\alpha_{p-1} }$. Next $(\delta\omega)_{\alpha \alpha_0...\alpha_p}=\omega_{\alpha_0\cdots\alpha_p}+\sum_{i=0}^p(-1)^{i+1}\omega_{\alpha\alpha_0\cdots\hat\alpha_i\cdots\alpha_p}=0$, From this we can infer that $\delta{\tau}=\omega_{\alpha_0\cdots\alpha_p}$. In fact $\tau$ gives a homotopy operator $K$ on the complex, $(K\omega)_{\alpha_0\cdots\alpha_{p-1} }=\sum_\alpha\rho_\alpha\omega_{\alpha\alpha_0\cdots\alpha_{p-1} }$. It satisfies $\delta K+K\delta=\mathrm{id}$. For the upper closed chain, $K$ is said to be the right inverse of $\delta$. double complex ------ Remember $K^{p,q}=C^p(\mathcal{U},\Omega^q)=\prod \Omega^q(U_{\alpha_0\cdots\alpha_p})$. The elements above it are called $p$-upper chains of $q$-form values. Treat $(p,q)$ as coordinates on $\mathbb{Z}^2$, then there is a commutative graph, The operations on the right side of the row are differential operators, and the operations on the column direction are differential operators. The exchange is because first limiting and then external differentiation is equal to first external differentiation and then limiting, and external differentiation has linearity. Let $K^n=\oplus_{p+q=n}K^{p,q}$, $D=D'+D''$, $D'=\delta$, $D''=(-1)^pd$. Then $D',D''$ are now exchanged with signs, so $D^2=0$, $\{K^\ast ,D\}$ becomes a complex. $C^\ast (\mathcal{U},\Omega^\ast )$ is called **Čech-de Rham complex**, The element above is abbreviated as $D$-uplink, and the cohomology is recorded as $H^\ast _D\{C^\ast (\mathcal{U},\Omega^\ast )\}$. Augment the chain complex, add $\Omega^p(M)$ at the $(-1,q)$ position, and limit the mapping $r$ to the right, The upward direction is the differential operator. Then add $0$ on the left to map to the right, then according to the previous proposition, all rows are consistent. **Proposition 1.2** (Generalized MV principle). *$r^\ast :H^\ast _{dR}(M)\approx H_D\{C^\ast (\mathcal{U},\Omega^\ast )\}$.* First of all, $r$ is a chain mapping, so $r^\ast $ is reasonably defined, $r^\ast [\omega]_d=[r\omega]_D$. Next, it is enough to prove that $r^\ast $ is both full and single. Just use the consistency of the rows to perform basic graph tracking. Go diagonally and cut off the excess components step by step. Application ---- It can be seen that if each row of the augmented bicomplex is exact, Then its $D$-cohomology is isomorphic to the cohomology of the initial sequence, that is, the augmented sequence. Take $C^p(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})=\prod\{U_{\alpha_0\cdots\alpha_p}\text{upper local constant function}\}$, Then there is a mapping $i:C^p(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})\rightarrow C^p(\mathcal{U},\Omega^0)$, And $\operatorname{Im}i=\ker d$. There is a complex $0\rightarrow C^0(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})\xrightarrow{\delta}C^1(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\cdots$, The cohomology is said to be the **Čech cohomology** of the open covering $\mathcal{U}$, which is a pure combinatorial object. Similar to the previous proof, if each column is exact, add augmenting rows, Then its cohomology is also isomorphic to the $D$-cohomology of the double complex. We take the complex constructed above as an augmented row. According to Poincaré’s lemma, If $\mathcal{U}$ is a good cover, then each column is indeed exact. Thus: **Theorem 1.3**. *$H^\ast (M)\cong H^\ast (\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})$.* So we have an inference that is not trivial a priori: **Corollary 1.4**. *Čech cohomology $H^\ast (\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})$ has nothing to do with the selection of good cover $\mathcal{U}$.* On the other hand, if the manifold has finite good coverage, $H^\ast (\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})$ has finite dimensions. Thus: **Corollary 1.5**. *The de Rham cohomology $H_{dR}^\ast (M)$ of a finite manifold $M$ is finite-dimensional.* In particular, compact manifolds always have finite good coverage, so de Rham cohomology is finite-dimensional.
