经典方程 -------- Recalling mathematical equations, the ones we most often come into contact with are the following equations: 1. $-\Delta u=f,$ **Poisson equation**. Discuss boundary value problems, The boundary conditions are of Dirichlet or Neumann type. 2. $u_{tt}-\Delta u=f,$ **Wave equation**. Discuss the Cauchy problem in the unbounded area, The initial boundary value problem is discussed in the bounded area. The initial conditions need to be given for $u$ and $u_t$, The boundary conditions are of Dirichlet or Neumann type. 3. $u_t-\Delta u=f,$ **Heat conduction equation**. The discussion content is basically the same wave equation, The initial conditions only need to be given for $u$. Considering the generalized form of the above equation, there is the following linear equation, The problems discussed correspond to the classic problems above and will not be repeated. The coefficients are all set to smooth. And satisfy the **ellipticity condition**: $\sum_{ij=1}^n a_{ij(x)}\xi_i\xi_j\ge \alpha \Vert\xi\Vert^2,$ $\,\forall\,\xi\neq 0.$ Let’s assume the coefficients are symmetric. 1. $Lu=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(x)u_{ij}+\sum_{i=1}^n b_i(x)u_i+c(x)u=f,$ **Second order elliptic equation**. 2. $u_{tt}-\sum_{i,j=1}^n\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(a_{ij}(t,x)\frac{\partial {}u}{\partial {}x_j})+\sum_{i=1}^n b_i(t,x)\frac{\partial {}u}{\partial {}x_i}+c(t,x)u=f,$ **Second-order hyperbolic equation**. 3. $u_{t}-\sum_{i,j=1}^n\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(a_{ij}(t,x)\frac{\partial {}u}{\partial {}x_j})+\sum_{i=1}^n b_i(t, x)\frac{\partial {}u}{\partial {}x_i}+c(t,x)u=f,$ **Second-order parabolic equation**. We move from considering **classical solutions** ($C^2$ solutions) to considering **weak solutions** (generalized function solutions) (Weak Formulation). From **Stokes formula**: $$\int_\Omega d\omega=\int_{\partial\Omega} \omega,$$ We have the **Gauss-Green formula**: $$\int_\Omega \partial_i fdx=\int_\Omega \nabla \cdot e_i fdx=\int_{\partial\Omega} n\cdot e_i \gamma fdS=\int_{\partial\Omega} n_i\gamma fdS.$$ where $\gamma$ is the trace, which makes the formula true for more functions. Then we have the **integration by parts formula**: $$\int_{\Omega} \partial_i fgdx+\int_\Omega f\partial_i gdx=\int_\Omega{\partial_i (fg)}dx=\int_{\partial\Omega} n_i\gamma(fg)dS.$$ To solve partial differential equations, a commonly used method is the **operator semigroup** method, This turns the problem into solving an infinite-dimensional ODE. dual operator -------- Consider the Dirichlet homogeneous boundary problem, $u\in H_0^1(\Omega).$ Apply the test function $v\in H_0^1(\Omega)$ to the equation: $$\begin{aligned} \int_\Omega vLudx=&{\color{blue}\sum_{i,j=1}^n\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(a_{ij}(x)u_jv)dx}-\sum_{i,j=1}^n\int_{\Omega}u_j\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(a_{ij}(x)v)dx\\ &+{\color{blue}\sum_{i=1}^n \int_\Omega \frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(b_i(x)uv)dx}-\sum_{i=1}^n \int_\Omega u\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(b_i(x)v)dx+\int_\Omega c(x)uvdx\\ =&-\sum_{i,j=1}^n\int_{\Omega}u_j\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(a_{ij}(x)v)dx-\sum_{i=1}^n \int_\Omega u\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(b_i(x)v)dx+\int_\Omega c(x)uvdx\\ =&{\color{blue}\sum_{i,j=1}^n\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_j}\left(u\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(a_{ij}(x)v)\right)dx}-\sum_{i,j=1}^n\int_{\Omega}u_j\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(a_{ij}(x)v)dx\\\ &-\sum_{i=1}^n \int_\Omega u\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(b_i(x)v)dx+\int_\Omega c(x)uvdx\\ =&\sum_{i,j=1}^n\int_{\Omega}u\frac{\partial^2 {} }{\partial {}x_i\partial {}x_j}(a_{ij}(x)v)dx-\sum_{i=1}^n \int_\Omega u\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(b_i(x)v)dx+\int_\Omega c(x)uvdx\\ =&\int_\Omega uL^*vdx. \end{aligned}$$ The Gauss-Green formula is used many times in the derivation. Since the function has homogeneous boundary conditions, all boundary terms (blue terms) disappear. From this we get the dual operator $L^\ast .$ Similarly, for hyperbolic and parabolic equations, we also have dual operators: $$M^\ast v=\frac{\partial {}^2v}{\partial {}t^2}-\sum_{i,j=1}^n\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(a_{ij}(x)v_j)-\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(b_i(x)v)+c(x)v,$$ $$M^\ast v=-\frac{\partial {}v}{\partial {}t}-\sum_{i,j=1}^n\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(a_{ij}(x)v_j)-\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_i}(b_i(x)v)+c(x)v.$$ The importance of the dual operator lies in the following important propositions from functional analysis: **Proposition 1**. *For Hilbert space $H,$ bounded linear operator $A:H\rightarrow H,$, the existence of problem $Ax=b,$ $\,\forall\,b\in H$ is equivalent to the uniqueness of problem $A^\ast y=0$ (that is, there is only zero solution).* **Proof:** Existence $\Rightarrow$ Uniqueness: $\,\forall\,y$ makes $A^\ast y=0,$ There exists $x$ such that $Ax=y.$ Therefore, $$0=(x,A^\ast y)=(Ax,y)=(y,y) \,\Rightarrow\, y=0.$$ Uniqueness $\Rightarrow$ Existence: For $b\in H,$ only need to find $x$ that satisfies: $$(Ax,y)=(b,y),\quad \forall y\in H.$$ Take $A^\ast :H\rightarrow B=\operatorname{Im} A^\ast ,$ is a continuous linear operator. It is injective because $A^\ast y=0$ has only zero solution. According to the inverse operator theorem, $(A^\ast )^{-1}:B\rightarrow H$ is also a continuous linear operator. Define continuous linear functional $l_b:B\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$: $$l_b(v)=(b,(A^\ast )^{-1}v)=(b,u),$$ Here $A^\ast u=v.$ is expressed by Riesz theorem, There exists $x\in B$ such that $l_b(v)=(x,v).$ Therefore for any $y\in H:$ $$l_b(A^\ast y)=(b,y)=(x,A^\ast y)=(Ax,y).$$ This shows that the $x\in B$ we found is exactly the solution of $Ax=b$. energy approach -------- ### Multiplier method With dual operators, in most cases we only have to deal with the proof of uniqueness, which is easy. Usually the energy method is used, and the uniqueness is explained by energy conservation through derivation. The energy method is often derived from the multiplier method. For elliptic equations, consider the energy form $\int uLu$ (potential energy); For the wave equation, consider the energy form $\int u_t Mu$ (kinetic energy); for the heat conduction equation, Consider the energy form $\int uMu$ (potential energy). Take the elliptic equation as an example. For $u\in H_0^1(\Omega),$ we have: $$\begin{aligned} -\int_\Omega uLudx=&\int_\Omega \sum_{i,j=1}^n a_{ij}(x)u_iu_jdx+\int_\Omega \sum_{i=1}^n\left(\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{\partial {}a_{ij} }{\partial {}x_j}-b_i(x)\right)u_iudx-\int_\Omega c(x)u^2dx\\ \ge &\int_\Omega \alpha \sum_{i=1}^n u_i^2dx-\int_\Omega C'\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\frac{\alpha}{2C'} u_i^2+\frac{C'}{2\alpha}u^2\right)dx-\int_\Omega C''u^2dx\\ \ge &\frac{\alpha}{2}\Vert\nabla u\Vert-C\Vert u\Vert. \end{aligned}$$ The inequality $2ab\le \varepsilon a^2+\frac{1}{\varepsilon} b^2.$ is used in the process This inequality is called Garding's inequality: **Theorem 2** (Garding inequality). *Suppose $\Omega$ is a bounded area in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with a smooth boundary. If $L$ is a second-order elliptical operator, then $\,\forall\,u\in H_0^1(\Omega),$ has the following inequality:* $$\operatorname{Re}(-Lu,u)_{L^2(\Omega)}\ge C_1\Vert u\Vert_{1}^2-C_2\Vert u\Vert^2.$$ The coefficients can be calculated specifically. When $C_2\le 0$, consider uniqueness for the problem $Lu=0$: $$C_1\Vert u\Vert_{1}^2-C_2\Vert u\Vert^2\le 0\,\Rightarrow\, u\equiv 0.$$ For linear equations, the uniqueness of $Lu=0$ is of course equivalent to the uniqueness of $Lu=f$. ### Characteristic issues Generally, $C_2$ is not necessarily non-positive. But for sufficiently large $\Lambda\in \mathbb{R},$ Considering $(-L+\Lambda)u=0$, you can forcibly adjust the right-hand side term in Garding's inequality to be non-negative, At this time, the solution to the problem is unique. Therefore, the inverse operator $(-L+\Lambda)^{-1}.$ can be defined in the image space We make the following derivation: $$\begin{aligned} C_1\Vert u\Vert^2_{1}&\le \left<{}(-L+\Lambda)u,u\right>\\ &\le \Vert(-L+\Lambda)u\Vert\Vert u\Vert\le \Vert(-L+\Lambda)u\Vert\Vert u\Vert_{H^1}\\ \Vert u\Vert_{1}&\le C_3\Vert(-L+\Lambda)u\Vert\\ \Vert(-L+\Lambda)^{-1}v\Vert_{1}&\le C_3 \Vert v\Vert \end{aligned}$$ Therefore $(-L+\Lambda)^{-1}$ is a bounded operator. Since $H_0^1\stackrel{c}\hookrightarrow L^2$ is a compact embedding, it is also a compact operator. Its eigenvalues can be listed, $\mu_i\rightarrow 0.$ Consider feature issues: $$\begin{aligned} -Lu&=\lambda u\\ (-L+\Lambda)u&=(\lambda+\Lambda)u\\ \frac{1}{\lambda+\Lambda}u&=(-L+\Lambda)^{-1}u=\mu u\\ \end{aligned}$$ Therefore, $-L$ eigenvalues are also listable, And $\mu_i=\frac{1}{\lambda_i+\Lambda}\rightarrow 0$ $\Rightarrow$ $\lambda_i\rightarrow \infty,$ The eigenvalues tend to infinity.
