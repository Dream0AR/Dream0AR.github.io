《微分流形》第七章-微分形式 "Differential Manifolds" Chapter 7 - Differential Forms

differential form -------- Recall $\,\forall\,f\in C^\infty(M),$ $(df)_p\in T_p^\ast M,$ is called the differential of $f$ at $p$. Locally we have $$ df|_U=\sum_i \frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x^i} fdx^i,\quad \frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x^i}f\in C^\infty(U)\,\Rightarrow\,df\in \mathcal{A}^1(M). $$ $\omega\in\Gamma(\Lambda^rT^\ast M)=\mathcal{A}^r(M),$ is called $r$ second-order differential form. Let $\mathcal{A}(M):=\bigoplus_{r=0}^m \mathcal{A}^r(M),$ It is called **differential form space**. It has an outer product $\wedge$ as a multiplicative structure and is a **layered algebra**. If $f\in C^\infty(M,N),$ Then it induces the pullback mapping $f^\ast :\Gamma(T_r^0(N))\rightarrow \Gamma(T_r^0(M)),$ $$ f^\ast (\Phi)(v_1,\cdots,v_r):=\Phi(f_\ast (v_1),\cdots,f_\ast (v_r)). $$ Examination coefficient: $$ (f^\ast \Phi)_{i_1\cdots i_r}=\Phi\left(f_\ast \frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_{i_1} },\cdots,f_\ast \frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x_{i_r} }\right)=\frac{\partial {}y^{\alpha_1}\circ f}{\partial {}x^{i_1} }\cdots\frac{\partial {}y^{\alpha_r}\circ f}{\partial {}x^{i_r} }\cdot \Phi_{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_r}\circ f. $$ It is indeed smooth, so $f^\ast \Phi\in \Gamma(T_r^0(M)).$ So $f^\ast :\mathcal{A}(N)\rightarrow \mathcal{A}(M),$ $f^\ast \sigma=\sum_{r=0}^n f^\ast \sigma_r,$ And $f^\ast (\omega\wedge\sigma)=f^\ast \omega\wedge f^\ast \sigma,$ $\,\forall\,\omega,\sigma\in \mathcal{A}(N).$ It is an algebraic homomorphism. external differential ------ Let $d:\mathcal{A}(M)\rightarrow \mathcal{A}(M)$ be a linear mapping, satisfying: 1. $d:\mathcal{A}^r(M)\rightarrow\mathcal{A}^{r+1}(M);$ 2. $\omega\in \mathcal{A}^r(M),$ $\sigma\in \mathcal{A}(M),$ $d(\omega\wedge\sigma)=d\omega\wedge\sigma+(-1)^r\omega\wedge d\sigma.$ 3. When $f\in \mathcal{A}^0(M)=C^\infty(M)$, $df$ is an ordinary differential, and $d^2f=0.$ Then $d$ is called the **external differential operator** on $M$, or simply **external differential**. **Lemma 1**. *External differential operators are local operators, that is, if $\omega_1|_U=\omega_2|_U,$ then $(d\omega_1)|_U=(d\omega_2)|_U.$* **Certificate:** Just state $\omega|_U=0\Rightarrow (d\omega)|_U=0.$ Just take the truncation function $f|_V\equiv 1,$ $\operatorname{supp}f\subset U$. So $f\omega=0.$ and thus $0=d(f\omega)=df\wedge \omega+f(d\omega),$ On $V$ we have $df=0,$ so $(d\omega)|_V=0.$ From the arbitrariness of $V$, we have $(d\omega)|_U=0.$ **Theorem 2**. *There is a unique external differential operator on $C^\infty$-manifold $M$.* **Proof:** Proof of uniqueness. From the truncation function, $d$ can induce the external differential operator on $U$. Take $f,$ in the lemma to $\omega\in \mathcal{A}(U),$ Local definition $(d\omega)|_V:=(d(f\omega))|_V.$ Show that $d$ is unique in the coordinate system: Take $(U,\varphi;x^i)\subset M,$ $\omega\in \mathcal{A}^r(M),$ $\omega|_U=adx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_r}.$ From the properties of external differential operators, $d(\omega|_U)=da\wedge dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_r}.$ By locality, $(d\omega)|_U=d(\omega|_U)=da\wedge dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_r},$ $d\omega$ has an exact expression in $U$, so $d$ is unique in the coordinate system. Thus, $d$ is also unique as a whole. Next, we will explain the existence of $d$. First, from the above paragraph, Just define $d\omega:=da\wedge dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_r}$ locally, $\omega=adx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_r}\in \mathcal{A}^r(U).$ In this way, it is naturally extended to the external differential operator. Since it is locally defined everywhere, it is also defined overall, And due to local uniqueness, it has nothing to do with the selection of the coordinate system. From the local definition of $d$, we naturally get the following lemma: **Lemma 3** (Poincaré’s Lemma). *$d^2=0.$ That is, $\,\forall\,\omega\in \mathcal{A}(M),$ $d(d\omega)=0.$* Suppose $\omega=Adx+Bdy+Cdz\in \mathcal{A}^1(\mathbb{R}^3)$ corresponds to $X=(A,B,C)\in \mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^3)$, When $\omega=df$, $X$ is $\nabla f.$ Then $d\omega$ corresponds to $\nabla\times X$. Let $\omega=Ady\wedge dz+Bdz\wedge dx+Cdx\wedge dy,$ Corresponds to $X=(A,B,C)\in \mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^3)$. Then $d\omega=(\nabla\cdot X)dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.$ Therefore $\nabla\times (\nabla f)=0$ is guaranteed by $d(df)=0$, $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times X)$ is guaranteed by $d(d\omega)=0$. **Theorem 4**. *Suppose $\omega\in \mathcal{A}^1(M),$ $X,Y\in \mathfrak{X}(M),$ then $d\omega(X,Y)=X(\omega(Y))-Y(\omega(X))-\omega([X,Y]).$* **Proof:** Suppose $\omega=fdg,$ then $d\omega=df\wedge dg.$ At this time $df\wedge dg(X,Y)=X(f)Y(g)-Y(f)X(g).$ The same result is obtained by calculating the right side, The theorem is proved.
