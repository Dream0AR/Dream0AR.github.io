generalized function -------- ### Basic definition $C_c^\infty\subset \mathscr{S}\subset C^\infty,$ Recorded as $\mathscr{D}\subset \mathscr{S}\subset \mathscr{E},$ Then $\mathscr{D}'\supset \mathscr{S}'\supset \mathscr{E}'.$ Note that the basic space used here is that the former is dense in the latter. The continuity of a generalized function is equivalent to boundedness. Note that boundedness is more special here, Because the basic space is not normed. But a sequence of norms can be obtained. An important dual property can be obtained from this property: $(\mathscr{D}')_c=\mathscr{E}'.$ ### Polished $\alpha_\varepsilon(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^n}\alpha(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}).$ The convolution $J_\varepsilon f:=f_\varepsilon=\alpha_\varepsilon\ast f$ is called the polish of $f$. Since $\alpha_\varepsilon$ is a radial function, we have: $$ \left<{}\alpha_\varepsilon\ast T,\varphi\right>=\left<{}T_x,\left<{}\alpha_\varepsilon(y),\varphi(x+y)\right>\right>=\left<{}T,\alpha_\varepsilon\ast \varphi\right>. $$ When $T\in \mathscr{D}'$, $\alpha_\varepsilon\ast T\in C^\infty.$ From Minkowski inequality, $\Vert\alpha_\varepsilon\ast f\Vert_{L^p}\le \Vert f\Vert_{L^p}.$ Minkowski's inequality can be derived from the discrete case or proven by functional analysis methods. When $\varepsilon\rightarrow 0$, We have $f_\varepsilon\xrightarrow{L^p} f.$ ### Seeking derivation A generalized function can "almost" always be written as a derivative of a normal function. Here the derivative is a generalized derivative, such as: $$ \left<{}T,\varphi\right>=\left<{}D^\alpha f,\varphi\right>=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\left<{}f,D^\alpha \varphi\right>. $$ The definition of derivation comes from integration by parts, derived from the Gauss-Green formula. $$ \int_\Omega \partial f\cdot g+f\cdot \partial g=\int_{\Omega} \partial(fg)=\int_{\partial \Omega} fg\cdot \gamma, $$ When one of $f, g$ takes zero on the boundary, the right end gets zero, and the equation about the left end is established. ### Fourier Transform For the rapid descending function, there is Fourier transformation: $$ F[f] (\xi)=\widehat{f}(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x)e^{-ix\cdot \xi}dx, $$ There is an inverse Fourier transformation: $$ f(x)=F^{-1}[\widehat{f}] (x):=(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\widehat{f}(\xi)e^{ix\cdot \xi}d\xi, $$ Meet: $$ \widehat{D^\alpha f}(\xi)=\xi^\alpha \widehat{f}(\xi),\quad D^\alpha=\frac{1}{i^{|\alpha|} }\partial^\alpha, $$ $$ \widehat{fg}=(2\pi)^{-n}\widehat{f}\ast \widehat{g}. $$ We have the classic proposition: $F:\mathscr{S}\cong \mathscr{S}$ is a continuous isomorphism. This directly induces $F:\mathscr{S}'\cong \mathscr{S}',$ $\left<{}F[T],\varphi\right>:=\left<{}T,F[\varphi]\right>.$ This is guaranteed by Fubini's theorem. We also have Plancherel's formula: $$ \int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\widehat{f}\overline{\widehat{g} }\bar{d}\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f \overline{g}dx,\quad \bar{d}\xi=(2\pi)^{-n}d\xi. $$ Taking $g=f,$, we get the Parseval identity for the continuous case. Sobolev space ---------- ### Sobolev’s inequality $$ \Vert u\Vert_{L^q}\le C|u|_{\dot{H}^{m,p} },\quad \frac{n}{q}=\frac{n}{p}-m; $$ $$ |u|_{C^\alpha}\le C|u|_{\dot{H}^{m,p} },\quad -\alpha=\frac{n}{p}-m,\,\alpha\in (0,1); $$ $$ \Vert u\Vert_{L^\infty}\le C\Vert u\Vert_{H^{m,p} },\quad 0>\frac{n}{p}-m; $$ $$ \Vert u\Vert_{L^\infty}\le C\Vert u\Vert_{H^{s} },\quad 0>\frac{n}{2}-s; $$ $$ \Vert u\Vert_{L^q}\le C\Vert u\Vert_{H^{m,p} },\quad \frac{n}{p}-m=0,\, q\ge p. $$ Regarding the last inequality, the left-hand dimension is $\frac{n}{q},$ The right-hand side dimension is $[\frac{n}{p}-m,\frac{n}{p}]=[0,m].$ In order to make the inequality true, The left-hand dimension must fall within this interval. Therefore, the condition $\frac{n}{q}\le \frac{n}{p}=m\,\Rightarrow\,q\ge p$ is necessary. We also have Poincaré’s inequality: $$ \Vert u\Vert_{L^p}\le d\Vert\nabla u\Vert_{L^p}=d|u|_{\dot{H}^{1,p} },\quad \,\forall\,u\in H_0^{1,p}(\Omega). $$ ### Theorem **Equivalent modular theorem**: extended by **Lions**, Functions in $H^{m,p}(\Omega)$ can always be extended to functions in $H^{m,p}(\mathbb{R}^n)$. definition $$ \Vert f\Vert_{\widetilde{H}^{m,p}(\Omega)}=\inf_{\widetilde{f}|_\Omega =f}\Vert\widetilde{f}\Vert_{H^{m,p}(\mathbb{R}^n)}, $$ Then with $\widetilde{f},$ obtained by extension of Lions, we have: $$ \Vert f\Vert_{H^{m,p}(\Omega)}\le \Vert f\Vert_{\widetilde{H}^{m,p}(\Omega)}\le \Vert\widetilde{f}\Vert_{H^{m,p}(\mathbb{R}^n)}\le C\Vert f\Vert_{H^{m,p}(\Omega)}, $$ **Compact embedding theorem**: When the conditions in Sobolev's inequality are strict, That is $\frac{n}{q}>\frac{n}{p}-m,$ At this time, the embedding of $H^{m,p}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{q}(\Omega)$ is tight, That is to map bounded sets into compact sets. **Trace Theorem**: $$ \Vert u\Vert_{H^{s-\frac{1}{2} }(\partial\Omega)}\le C\Vert u\Vert_{H^s(\Omega)},\quad s>\frac{1}{2} $$ A simple corollary that is most commonly applied is: $$ \Vert u\Vert_{L^2(\partial \Omega)}\le C\Vert u\Vert_{H^1(\Omega)}. $$ Regarding the dual space: It should be noted that $H^{-1}(\Omega):=(H_0^1(\Omega))^\ast ,$ It is not the dual space of $H^1(\Omega)$. Only when $\Omega=\mathbb{R}^n$, $H_0^1(\mathbb{R}^n)=H^1(\mathbb{R}^n).$ At the same time, expressed by Riesz theorem, $H^{-1}(\Omega)\cong H_0^1(\Omega),$ are not equal. Because the former is regarded as the subspace of $\mathscr{D}'(\Omega)$, and $L^2(\Omega)$ is the subspace, The functional is taken as $L^2$ inner product; while the latter uses $H^1$ inner product as the functional, which leads to a difference in the dual space, But after all, they are isomorphic correspondences. PDE --- ### Basic definition Second-order elliptic equation: $$ Lu=\sum \partial_i(a_{ij}u_j)+\sum b_i u_i+cu. $$ Second-order hyperbolic equation: $$ Mu=u_{tt}-\sum \partial_i (a_{ij}u_j)-\sum b_{0j}u_{ti}+\sum b_iu_i+b_0 u_t+cu. $$ It can be recorded as $Mu=u_{tt}-M_1u_t+M_2u.$ Second order parabolic equation: $$ Nu=u_{t}-\sum \partial_i (a_{ij}u_j)+\sum b_i u_i+cu. $$ It can be recorded as $Nu=u_t-N_1u.$ The coefficient $a_{ij}$ is symmetric and satisfies the ellipticity condition: $\sum a_{ij}\xi_i\xi_j\ge \alpha|\xi|^2.$ The conditions are consistent when time terms are involved. ### Energy Method For elliptic equations, $\left<{}u,Lu\right>$ gives Garding's inequality; $\left<{}u_t,Mu\right>,$ of hyperbolic equation The $\left<{}u,Nu\right>$ of the parabolic equation gives an energy estimate. ### Galerkin method Take a set of bases of the basic space $\{\varphi_i\},$ Consider $u_m=\sum a_i\varphi_i,$ Solution $$ \left<{}Lu_m-f,\varphi_i\right>=0,\quad i=1,\cdots,m. $$ This is reduced to the form of a linear equation system $Ax=b$, and just solve for the coefficients. The solvability here is guaranteed by Garding's inequality. $\frac{A+A^T}{2}$ is positive definite, and $A$ is reversible. The quadratic form in the formula can be written in the form of $x^T\frac{A+A^T}{2}x=\left<{}-Lu,u\right>$, When $C_2\le 0$, the positive definiteness is inferred. For the Galerkin method involving time terms, it is necessary to consider $u_m=\sum a_i(t)\varphi_i.$ at this time $$ \left<{}Mu_m,\varphi_i\right>=\left<{}f,\varphi_i\right>,\quad i=1,\cdots,m $$ is $m$ ODEs, which are always solvable. Specifically, $$ \sum \left<{}\varphi_j,\varphi_i\right>a_j''-\left<{}M_1\varphi_j,\varphi_i\right>a_j'+\left<{}M_2\varphi_j,\varphi_i\right>a_j=\left<{}f,\varphi_i\right>. $$ Similarly, for parabolic equations, the question is: $$ \left<{}Nu_m,\varphi_i\right>=\sum \left<{}\varphi_j,\varphi_i\right>a_j'-\left<{}N_1\varphi_j,\varphi_i\right>a_j=\left<{}f,\varphi_i\right>. $$ ### Operator semigroup Consider the problem $u_t=N_1u,$ Formally, $e^{N_1t}u_0$ gives the solution. Specifically, Consider $N_\lambda:=\lambda N_1(\lambda-N_1)^{-1},$ Define $e^{N_1t}:=\lim\limits_{\lambda\rightarrow+\infty}e^{N_\lambda t}$ to make the representation strict.
