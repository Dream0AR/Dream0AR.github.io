physical meaning -------- *The following is this non-physics student’s understanding of the physical meaning of the theorem after roughly reading the physics literature and consulting the website. It may be unprofessional, please forgive me.* We all know that there is universal gravitation, and objects in space will exert a force on surrounding objects. This force field is called **gravitational field**. The field itself should have energy, which is called **ADM mass**, Defined by three physicists and named after their initials. Intuitively, placing an object on a flat cloth will cause the entire cloth to distort (sink near the object, Slightly sinking at infinity, the effect shrinks with distance), this is an intuitive model of the gravitational field. Note that after twisting this cloth, it forms a two-dimensional non-trivial surface. We can discuss the geometric structure based on the induced metric, and the knowledge of Riemannian geometry is introduced. In order to explore the energy of an isolated system, assume that there is only this one object in the space, Then its gravitational field should have almost no effect at infinity, which is physically called **asymptotically flat spacetime**. Still taking the above cloth as an example, it is close to the plane at infinity, Therefore, it is mathematically consistent with the definition of space as **asymptotically flat**. The expression of the positive mass theorem in physics is: *If the material distribution of an isolated system satisfies the main energy condition, Then its ADM mass is non-negative.* Mathematically, the connection between physical quantities and geometric quantities can be established. Under certain circumstances, The principal energy condition can deduce the condition that the curvature of the space * quantity is non-negative *. Similar to Gauss's theorem, The energy within the sphere should be detectable on the sphere, defining the ADM mass: $$ E=\frac{1}{4(n-1)\omega_{n-1} }\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow \infty}\int_{S_\sigma}\sum_{i,j}(g_{ij,i}\nu_j-g_{ii,j}\nu_j)d\xi(\sigma). $$ Here $S_\sigma=\{|x|=\sigma\}.$ The comma represents the partial derivative, Such as $g_{ij,k}:=\partial_kg_{ij}.$ $\nu=\sigma^{-1}x$ is the unit outer normal vector of $S_\sigma$. The integrability of this formula is guaranteed by the assumption of asymptotic flatness, from which the energy of the gravitational field is obtained. The positive mass theorem in mathematics is to prove that $E\ge 0.$ under the above assumptions. In particular, $E=0$ is only obtained in Euclidean space. In the physical sense, that is, if the gravitational field energy is zero, there will be no distortion of the space. According to the previous example, no object is placed on the cloth. This theorem is not as obvious as imagined in the physical sense, because for an isolated system, The gravitational binding energy between substances is negative. In Newton's gravity theory, The energy of the gravitational field generated by an isolated point mass is not only negative, but can also tend to negative infinity. The total energy of such an isolated system can also be negative. So not only is the physical aspect of the positive mass theorem not an obvious result, Or is it an important feature that distinguishes general relativity from Newton's gravity theory? On the other hand, it is also of great significance to deduce the space triviality from the special case $E=0$. For three-dimensional space, If the ADM mass is zero, it must be Euclidean space, and space-time itself must be Minkowski space-time. This shows that Minkowski spacetime is not only the lowest energy among all spacetimes, And it is the only space-time with the lowest energy value. This is of great significance to ensure the stability of Minkowski space-time. It also means that the ADM mass of all non-trivial asymptotically flat spacetimes is positive. This is the meaning of the word "positive" in the positive mass theorem. asymptotically flat manifold ---------- Take the Riemannian manifold $(M^n,g),$ and call it **asymptotically flat**, if there is a compact set $K\subset M,$ Such that $\Phi:M\setminus K\approx \mathbb{R}^n\setminus \overline{B}_1$ is a diffeomorphism. At the same time, in the coordinate system determined by $\Phi$, $g_{ij}(x)=\delta_{ij}+O(|x|^{-p}),$ $p>\frac{n-2}{2};$ $|R(g)|=O(|x|^{-q}),$ $q>n.$ In addition, we need to find: $$ |x||g_{ij,k}(x)|+|x|^2|g_{ij,kl}(x)|=O(|x|^{-p}). $$ Here the comma refers to the partial guide. This defines the total energy of $M$. Recall the quantity curvature expression: $$ \begin{aligned} R(g)&=g^{ij}R_{ij}=g^{ij}g^{kl}R_{ikjl}\\ &=g^{ij}g^{kl}\left<{}D_{\partial_k}D_{\partial_i}\partial_j-D_{\partial_i}D_{\partial_k}\partial_j,\partial_l\right>\\ &=g^{ij}(\Gamma_{ij,k}^k-\Gamma_{kj,i}^k+\Gamma_{kl}^k\Gamma_{ij}^l-\Gamma_{il}^k\Gamma_{kj}^l) \end{aligned} $$ Contact coefficient expression: $$ \Gamma_{ij}^k=\frac{1}{2}g^{km}(g_{im,j}+g_{jm,i}-g_{ij,m})=O(|x|^{-p-1}), $$ Note here that the estimate of the inverse matrix is also $g^{ij}(x)=\delta^{ij}+O(|x|^{-r}).$ The derivation of the inverse matrix has the following formula: $$ \partial_kg^{ij}=-g^{ir}g_{rs,k}g^{sl}=O(|x|^{-p-1}), $$ When the terms containing $g^{ij}$ are summed over $i,j$, only $i=j$ accounts for the main part, so the following estimation is true: $$ \begin{aligned} g^{ij}(\Gamma_{ij,k}^k-\Gamma_{kj,i}^k)&=\frac{g^{ij}g^{km} }{2}(g_{im,jk}+g_{jm,ik}-g_{ij,mk}-g_{km,ji}-g_{jm,ki}+g_{kj,mi})+O(|x|^{-2p-2})\\ &=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,k}(g_{ik,ik}-g_{ii,kk}-g_{kk,ii}+g_{ki,ki})+O(|x|^{-2p-2})\\ &=\sum_{i,j}(g_{ij,ij}-g_{ii,jj})+O(|x|^{-2p-2}) \end{aligned} $$ Combined with $\Gamma_{kl}^k\Gamma_{ij}^l-\Gamma_{il}^k\Gamma_{kj}^l=O(|x|^{-2p-2}),$ We get $R(g)=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij,ij}-g_{ii,jj}+O(|x|^{-2p-2}).$ Since $2p+2>n, $ the remainder is absolutely integrable at infinity. And $R(g)$ itself is integrable, Therefore $\sum_{i,j}g_{ij,ij}-g_{ii,jj}$ can be integrated. Therefore, by integrating by parts, the following limit exists: $$ \lim_{\sigma\rightarrow \infty}\int_{S_\sigma}\sum_{i,j}(g_{ij,i}\nu_j-g_{ii,j}\nu_j)d\xi. $$ Here $S_\sigma=\{|x|=\sigma\},$ $\nu=\sigma^{-1}x$ is the external normal vector of $S_\sigma$ unit. The total energy is defined according to the following formula $E=E(M,g),$ $$ E=\frac{1}{4(n-1)\omega_{n-1} }\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow \infty}\int_{S_\sigma}\sum_{i,j}(g_{ij,i}\nu_j-g_{ii,j}\nu_j)d\xi. $$ The basic content of the positive mass theorem is that if there is $R(g)\ge 0,$ everywhere, then $E\ge 0.$ At the same time, $E=0$ if and only if $(M,g)$ is isomorphic to Euclidean space. Yamabe problem ---------- ### Conformal class Define its **conformal class** for the metric $g,$: $$ [g]=\{e^ug,u\in C^\infty(M)\}. $$ 1960, Yamabe claims to have proved the following proposition: Given a compact Riemannian manifold $(M,g),$ $n\ge 3,$ There is a metric conformal to $g$, with constant curvature. However, a bigger problem arises in the proof, The proposition thus became a problem, called Yamabe's problem. Twenty-four years later, Schoen gave a proof. If $\widetilde{g}=e^ug,$ we calculate the change of the geometric quantity. First of all, it is easy to get: $$ \widetilde{\Gamma}_{ij}^m=\Gamma_{ij}^m+\frac{1}{2}(\delta_j^m \partial_i u+\delta_i^m\partial_ju-g^{mk}g_{ij}\partial_k u). $$ Take the normal coordinate system at point $x$ under measure $g$, recall $$ \widetilde{R}_{ijkl}=\widetilde{g}_{lm}(\partial_j \widetilde{\Gamma}_{ik}^m-\partial_i\widetilde{\Gamma}_{jk}^m+\widetilde{\Gamma}_{ik}^r\widetilde{\Gamma}_{jr}^m-\widetilde{\Gamma}_{jk}^r\widetilde{\Gamma}_{ir}^m). $$ Since $\widetilde{g}(x)_{lm}=e^{u(x)}\delta_{lm},$ $\partial_k g_{ij}(x)=0,$ $$ \begin{aligned} \delta_{lm}(\partial_j \widetilde{\Gamma}_{ik}^m(x)-\partial_i\widetilde{\Gamma}_{jk}^m(x))=&\delta_{lm}(\partial_j\Gamma_{ik}^m(x)-\partial_i\Gamma_{jk}^m(x))\\ &+\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{il}\partial_{jk}u(x)+\delta_{jk}\partial_{il}u(x)-\delta_{jl}\partial_{ik}u(x)-\delta_{ik}\partial_{jl}u(x)) \end{aligned} $$ $$ \begin{aligned} \delta_{lm}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{ik}^r\widetilde{\Gamma}_{jr}^m-\widetilde{\Gamma}_{jk}^r\widetilde{\Gamma}_{ir}^m)=&\frac{\delta_{lm} }{4}\left\{(\delta_{i}^r\partial_k u+\delta_k^r\partial_iu-\delta^{rs}\delta_{ik}\partial_su)(\delta_{j}^m\partial_r u+\delta_r^m\partial_ju-\delta^{mt}\delta_{jr}\partial_tu)\right.\\ &\left.-(\delta_{j}^r\partial_k u+\delta_k^r\partial_ju-\delta^{rs}\delta_{jk}\partial_su)(\delta_{i}^m\partial_r u+\delta_r^m\partial_iu-\delta^{mt}\delta_{ir}\partial_tu)\right\}\\ =&\frac{1}{4}\left\{(\delta_{i}^r\partial_k u+\delta_k^r\partial_iu-\delta_{ik}\partial^ru)(\delta_{lj}\partial_r u+\delta_{lr}\partial_ju-\delta_{jr}\partial_lu)\right.\\ &\left.-(\delta_{j}^r\partial_k u+\delta_k^r\partial_ju-\delta_{jk}\partial^ru)(\delta_{li}\partial_r u+\delta_{lr}\partial_iu-\delta_{ir}\partial_lu)\right\}\\ =&\frac{1}{4}\left\{(\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}-\delta_{jl}\delta_{ik})\partial_ru\partial^ru+(\delta_{jl}\partial_ku\partial_iu+\delta_{ik}\partial_ju\partial_lu-\delta_{il}\partial_ku\partial_ju-\delta_{jk}\partial_iu\partial_lu)\right\} \end{aligned} $$ Finally got $$ \widetilde{R}=e^{-u}\left(R-(n-1)\Delta_gu-\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{4}|\nabla u|_g^2\right). $$ When $n=2$, the Yamabe problem is whether there exists $\lambda\in \mathbb{R},$ $u\in C^\infty(M),$ such that $$ -\Delta_gu+R=\lambda e^u. $$ This equation is not particularly difficult. When $n\ge 3$, It is a good problem for nonlinear analysis on manifolds. Let $e^u=v^{\frac{4}{n-2} },$ $v$ is a smooth positive function, then the equation becomes as follows: $$ -\Delta_g v+\frac{n-2}{4(n-1)}Rv=\frac{n-2}{4(n-1)}\widetilde{R}v^{2^\ast -1}. $$ Where $2^\ast =\frac{2n}{n-2}.$ Yamabe's problem is reduced to whether there exists $\lambda\in \mathbb{R}$ and a smooth positive function $u,$ on $M$ make $$ -\Delta_g u+\frac{n-2}{4(n-1)}Ru=\lambda u^{2^\ast -1}. $$ This turns a purely geometric problem into a purely analytical problem. ### Conformal Laplace operator Inspired by the previous formula, we define the conformal Laplace operator: $$ L:=-\Delta+c_nR,\quad c_n=\frac{n-2}{4(n-1)}. $$ It is conformally invariant and can be expressed specifically as the following proposition: **Proposition 1**. *Suppose $(M,g)$ is a compact Riemannian manifold. Let $\varphi\in C^\infty(M),$ then $n\ge 3$, for $\widetilde{g}=e^{\varphi}g=\psi^{\frac{4}{n-2} }g,$* $$ L_{\widetilde{g} }u=\psi^{-(2^\ast -1)}L_g(u\psi). $$ **Certificate:** $$ \begin{aligned} \Delta_{\widetilde{g} }u&=\frac{e^{-\frac{n\varphi}{2} } }{\sqrt{g} }\partial_i(e^{\frac{n-2}{2}\varphi}g^{ij}\sqrt{g}\partial_ju)\\ &=e^{-\varphi}\left(\Delta_gu+\frac{n-2}{2}\partial_i\varphi g^{ij}\partial_ju\right) \end{aligned} $$ Among them $\partial_i\varphi g^{ij} \partial_j u=\left<{}D\varphi,Du\right>_g.$ Thus, $$ \Delta_{\widetilde{g} }u=\psi^{-\frac{4}{n-2} }\left(\Delta_gu+2\frac{\left<{}D\psi,Du\right>_g}{\psi}\right)=\psi^{-(2^*-1)}(\psi \Delta_g u+2\left<{}D\psi,Du\right>_g) . $$ Thus, $$ \begin{aligned} L_{\widetilde{g} }u&=\psi^{-(2^*-1)}\left(-\psi\Delta_gu-2\left<{}D\psi,Du\right>_g+\frac{n-2}{4(n-1)}Ru\psi -u\Delta_g\psi\right)\\ &=\psi^{-(2^*-1)}(-\Delta_g(u\psi)+c_nRu\psi)=\psi^{-(2^*-1)}L_g(u\psi).\\ \end{aligned} $$ Next we define the concept of conformally flat. The Riemannian manifold $(M,g)$ is called **conformally flat**, If $\,\forall\,x\in M,$ exists an open neighborhood $V,$ of $x$ $u\in C^\infty(M),$ Make $e^ug$ be flat on $V$. positive mass theorem ---------- ### Measurement Transformation With these preparations in place, the proof of the positive mass theorem can begin. First we transform the measurement: **Proposition 2**. *Suppose $(M,g)$ is an asymptotically flat manifold, $p>\frac{n-2}{2},$ $q>n.$ Assume $R(g)\ge 0,$ then $\,\forall\,\varepsilon>0,$ $\,\exists\,\overline{g}$ makes $(M,\overline{g})$ still asymptotically flat at infinity, And it is conformally flat, At the same time $R(\overline{g})\equiv 0,$ $E(\overline{g})\le E(g)+\varepsilon.$* **Proof:** First, we can set $R(g)\equiv 0,$ and consider the conformal Laplace equation $Lu=0,$ Find the solution $u=1+A|x|^{2-n}+O(|x|^{1-n}),$ $A\le 0.$ So at infinity, $u\in(0,1).$ At this time, the quantitative curvature of measure $u^{\frac{4}{n-2} }g$ is zero, And the total energy is given by $E(g)+A\le E(g)$. Therefore, this measure can be used instead of $g$. Next we turn $g$ into a Euclidean metric at infinity. Take the truncation function $\Psi_\sigma(x)$ to satisfy the following properties: $$ \left\{\begin{aligned} &\Psi_\sigma(x)=1,\quad |x|\le \sigma,\\ &\text{单调递减, 且} \sigma |\Psi_\sigma'|+\sigma^2|\Psi''_\sigma|\le c,\\ &\Psi_\sigma(x)=0,\quad |x|\ge 2\sigma. \end{aligned}\right. $$ Consider the metric $^{(\sigma)}g:=\Psi_\sigma g+(1-\Psi_\sigma)\delta,$ $\delta=\delta_{ij}dx^idx^j$ is the Euclidean metric. Note that when $\sigma$ is sufficiently large, Consistently for $\sigma$ we have ${}^{(\sigma)}g=\delta+O(|x|^{-p}),$ And $R\left({}^{(\sigma)}g\right)=O(|x|^{-2-p}).$ In particular, we have: $$ \int_M \left|R\left({}^{(\sigma)}g\right)\right|^{\frac{n}{2} }d\omega_g=O(\sigma^{-\frac{np}{2} }). $$ Therefore, for sufficiently large $\sigma,$, the equation $L_\sigma u_\sigma=0,$ $u_\sigma>0,$ The unique solution of $u_\sigma\rightarrow 1$. Therefore, the metric ${}^{(\sigma)}\overline{g}=u_\sigma^{\frac{4}{n-2} }{}^{(\sigma)}g$ is quantitatively flat, And it is conformally flat at infinity. Finally, $\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow \infty}E({}^{(\sigma)}\overline{g})=E(g)$ is enough, In this way, a sufficiently large $\sigma$ can satisfy the proposition requirements. From the consistent attenuation estimate of $u_\sigma,{}^{(\sigma)}g$, given $\varepsilon>0,$ There is $r_0$ that does not depend on $\sigma$ such that $$ \left|E\left({}^{(\sigma)}\overline{g}\right)-\frac{1}{4(n-1)\omega_{n-1} }\int_{S_{r_0} }\sum_{i,j}{}^{(\sigma)}\overline{g}_{ij,i}\nu_j-{}^{(\sigma)}\overline{g}_{ii,j}\nu_jd\xi\right|\le \frac{\varepsilon}{3} $$ $$ \left|E(g)-\frac{1}{4(n-1)\omega_{n-1} }\int_{S_{r_0} }\sum_{i,j}g_{ij,i}\nu_j-g_{ii,j}\nu_jd\xi\right|\le \frac{\varepsilon}{3} $$ Since we have transformed $g$ into a metric with zero curvature, on the compact subset, $\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow\infty}u_\sigma=1.$ Therefore, the integral terms in the above two equations are approximate. Therefore, for sufficiently large $\sigma,$ $|E({}^{(\sigma)}\overline{g})-E(g)|<\varepsilon.$ This completes the proof of the proposition. If $(M,g)$ is asymptotically flat and conformally flat at infinity, Then $g_{ij}=h^{\frac{4}{n-2} }(x)\delta_{ij},$ $|x|$ is sufficiently large, And $h(x)\rightarrow 1.$ If $R(g)\equiv 0,$ Recall the definition of conformal Laplace, It is known that $h$ is a harmonic function when $|x|$ is sufficiently large, The specific form is $h=1+E|x|^{2-n}+O(|x|^{1-n}).$ Here we normalize the energy, Let $E$ be exactly the total energy of metric $h^{\frac{4}{n-2} }\delta$. From this we can always assume that the measure in the above proposition is of this form, It is no longer expressed in the form $g=\delta+O(|x|^{-p})$. ### Minimal surface method **Theorem 3** (Positive mass theorem). *Suppose $(M,g)$ is asymptotically flat, $p>\frac{n-2}{2},$ $q>n,$ $R(g)\ge 0,$ then $E(g)\ge 0,$ and $E(g)=0$ if and only if $(M,g)$ is equidistant from $(\mathbb{R}^n,\delta).$* We give the proof when $n\le 7$. For the case of $n\ge 8$, The problem of minimal surface singularities can be overcome through additional techniques. The case of $E(g)=0$ will not be discussed for the time being. **Proof:** Contraproof, assuming $E(g)<0,$ then from the proposition in the previous section, we can assume $R(g)\equiv 0,$ $g_{ij}=h^{\frac{4}{n-2} }\delta_{ij},$ $h(x)=1+E|x|^{2-n}+O(|x|^{1-n}).$ Now it is more convenient to transform $R(g)>0$. We take the positive smooth function $v$ to be sufficiently small, And decays rapidly at infinity. Take the solution $Lu=v,$ $u=1+\delta|x|^{2-n}+O(|x|^{1-n}),$ $\delta$ is sufficiently small. Just replace the metric with $u^{\frac{4}{n-2} }g$. This also ensures the negativity of energy. Let's calculate the divergence of the unit vector field $\eta=h^{-\frac{2}{n-2} }\partial_n$ with respect to $g$. $$ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{div}_g(\eta)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g} }\partial_i\left(\sqrt{g}\eta^i\right)=h^{-\frac{2n}{n-2} }\partial_n\left(h^{\frac{2n}{n-2} }h^{-\frac{2}{n-2} }\right)\\ &=\frac{2(n-1)}{n-2}E\partial_n\left(|x|^{2-n}\right)+O\left(|x|^{-n}\right)\\ &=-2(n-1)E\frac{x^n}{|x|^n}+O\left(|x|^{-n}\right) \end{aligned} $$ In particular, we see that $\,\exists\,a_0\in \mathbb{R},$ $\operatorname{div}_g(\eta) \begin{cases} >0,&x^n\ge a_0\\<0,&x^n\le -a_0 \end{cases}.$ Let $\sigma$ be sufficiently large, $\Gamma_{\sigma,a}$ represents the $n-2$ dimensional sphere $$ \Gamma_{\sigma,a}:=\{x=(x',x^n):|x'|=\sigma,x^n=a\}. $$ $C_\sigma$ represents the $n-1$ dimensional cylinder $$ C_\sigma:=\{(x',x_n):|x'|=\sigma\}. $$ Orient $\Gamma_{\sigma,a}$ as the boundary of the cylindrical part below it in $C_\sigma$. Let $\Sigma_{\sigma,a}$ be the $n-1$ dimensional minimal surface, $\partial{\Sigma_{\sigma,a} }=\Gamma_{\sigma,a}.$ $C_\sigma$ frames the inner area $\Omega_\sigma,$ $\Sigma_{\sigma,a}\subset \Omega_\sigma.$ $n\le 7$, There are no singular points on the minimal surface $\Sigma_{\sigma,a}$. $\,\forall\,\sigma,$ definition $$ V(\sigma)=\min\{\operatorname{Vol}(\Sigma_{\sigma,a}):a\in [-a_0,a_0]\}, $$ Here $a\mapsto\operatorname{Vol}(\Sigma_{\sigma,a})$ is a continuous function. Assert that there exists $a=a(\sigma)\in (-a_0,a_0),$ $\operatorname{Vol}(\Sigma_{\sigma,a})=V(\sigma).$ In order to illustrate $a(\sigma)a_0-\delta\}, $$ $\delta$ is sufficiently small such that $\operatorname{div}_g(\eta)>0,$ $x^n>a_0-\sigma.$ Let us explain $U_{\sigma,a}=\varnothing.$ If $U_{\sigma,a}\neq \varnothing,$ $a>a_0-\delta.$ According to the integral formula by parts, And since $\eta$ is longitudinally tangent to $C_\sigma$, $$ \int_{U_{\sigma,a} }\operatorname{div}_g(\eta)d\mathcal{H}^n=\int_{\Sigma_{\sigma,a}\cap \{x^n\ge a_0-\delta\} }\left<{}\eta,\nu\right>_gd\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega_{\sigma,a}\cap\{x^n=a_0-\delta\})>0, $$ Where $\nu$ is the unit normal direction of $\Sigma_{\sigma,a}$. According to Schwartz inequality, $$ \operatorname{Vol}(\Omega_{\sigma,a}\cap \{x^n=a_0-\delta\})<\operatorname{Vol}(\Sigma_{\sigma,a}\cap \{x^n\ge a_0-\delta\}). $$ In this way, $\Sigma:=\partial(\Omega_{\sigma,a}\cap \{x^n+\operatorname{div}_M Z+(\operatorname{div}_MX)^2+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|(D_{e_i}X)^{\perp}|^2-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}\left<{}e_i,D_{e_j}X\right>\left<{}e_j,D_{e_i}X\right>. $$ Where $Z=D_{\partial_t}\frac{\partial {}F_t}{\partial {}t}$ is the changing acceleration vector field. Among them $$ \operatorname{div}_MX=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left<{}D_{e_i}X,e_i\right>, $$ $\{e_i\}$ is the orthogonal basis of $\Sigma$ tangent space. Do orthogonal decomposition $X=\widetilde{X}+\varphi\nu,$ $Z=\widetilde{Z}+\psi\nu.$ Since $\Sigma$ is extremely small, $\operatorname{div}_M \chi\nu=0,$ For any function $\chi.$ we have: $$ F_X=-\varphi^2\operatorname{Ric}(\nu,\nu)-\varphi^2\Vert B\Vert^2+\Vert\nabla \varphi\Vert^2+G, $$ $$ \begin{aligned} G=&-2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\varphi\left<{}R(\widehat{X},e_i)\nu,e_i\right>-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\varphi\left<{}R(\widehat{X},e_i)\widehat{X},e_i\right>+\operatorname{div}_M\widehat{Z}+(\operatorname{div}_M\widehat{X})^2\\ &-2B(\nabla \varphi,\widehat{X})+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}B(e_i,\widehat{X})^2-2\varphi\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}b_{ij}\widehat{X}_{i;j}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}\widehat{X}_{i;j}\widehat{X}_{j;i}. \end{aligned} $$ We discuss the above formulas under an orthogonal frame. Among them, $B(-,-)$ represents the second basic type, that is, $B(V,W)=\left<{}D_VW,\nu\right>.$ The semicolon refers to finding covariant derivatives, and the metric is the induced metric. The quantities related to $Z,\widehat{X}$ need to be expressed as bounded quantities. If $D\subset \Sigma$ is a bounded area, $$ \int_DGd\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\partial D}\left\{(\operatorname{div}\widehat{X})\left<{}\widehat{X},\eta\right>-\sum_{i,j}\widehat{X}_{i;j}\widehat{X}_j\eta_i-2\varphi\sum_{i,j}b_{ij}\widehat{X}_i\eta_j+\left<{}\widehat{Z},\eta\right>\right\}d\mathcal{H}^{n-2} $$ $\eta$ is the unit outer normal direction of $\partial D$ in $\Sigma$. The middle term disappears because of the Gauss-Codazzi equation and the Ricci formula. For $\sigma>0,$ Let $D_\sigma=\Omega_\sigma\cap \Sigma.$ given the attenuation conditions of $f, h$, Each boundary term decays faster than $\sigma^{2-n}$, so the boundary term tends to zero, When $\sigma\rightarrow\infty$. Thus $$ \int_\Sigma (\operatorname{Ric}(\nu,\nu)+\Vert B\Vert^2)\varphi^2d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\le \int_\Sigma \Vert\nabla \varphi\Vert^2d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}. $$ Here $\varphi=\left<{}X,\nu\right>.$ Since $X$ can be chosen arbitrarily in the compact set, $\varphi$ is fixed to $$ \varphi=\alpha\left<{}\partial_n,\nu\right>=\alpha h^{\frac{2}{n-2} }(1+|\nabla f|^2)^{-\frac{1}{2} }. $$ Since $\varphi-\alpha=O(|x'|^{2-n}),$ it has finite energy. Therefore, $\varphi$ can be chosen arbitrarily as long as $\varphi-\alpha$ has compact support or finite energy. From the Gauss equation, $$ \operatorname{Ric}(\nu,\nu)+\Vert B\Vert^2=\frac{1}{2}R_M-\frac{1}{2}R_{\Sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\Vert B\Vert^2. $$ $R_{\Sigma}$ is the quantitative curvature of $\Sigma$ under the induced metric. To complete the proof, first assume $n=3,$ $\varphi\equiv 1.$ In this way $\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}R_{\Sigma}d\mathcal{H}^2>0.$ Now $\frac{1}{2}R_{\Sigma}$ is the Gauss curvature of $\Sigma$, The attenuation estimates of $f,h$ indicate that the geodesic curvature of $\partial D_{\sigma}$ converges to $2\pi.$ Apply the Gauss-Bonnet formula to $D_\sigma$, let $\sigma\rightarrow \infty,$ $$ \frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}R_{\Sigma}d\mathcal{H}^2=2\pi \chi(\Sigma)-2\pi. $$ Since $\Sigma$ is an open surface, the right-hand term of $\chi(\Sigma)\le 1,$ is not positive. This is contradictory to the aforementioned inequality, so the proposition $n=3$ holds. For the case of $n\ge 4$, since the induced metric $\overline{g}$ satisfies: $$ \overline{g}_{ij}=h(x',f(x'))^{\frac{4}{n-2} }(\delta_{ij}+\partial_if\partial_jf)=\delta_{ij}+O(|x|^{2-n}), $$ $(\Sigma,\overline{g})$ is asymptotically flat and has zero energy. From the previous inequality and Gauss equation, and $R_M\ge 0,$ It shows that the minimum eigenvalue of $L_{\overline{g} }$ is positive in the compact region of $\Sigma$, Because $c(n)=\frac{n-2}{4(n-1)}<\frac{1}{2},$ $n\ge 3.$ Linear theory allows us to solve $L_{\overline{g} }u=0,$ $u>0,$ on $\Sigma$ At infinity $u\rightarrow 1.$ At the same time, $u$ has an expansion $$ u(x')=1+E_0|x'|^{3-n}+O(|x'|^{2-n}). $$ In particular, $u-1$ has finite energy on $\Sigma$, take $\varphi=u.$ by $R_M>0,$ $$ -\int_{\Sigma}R_{\Sigma}u^2d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}<2\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^2d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\le c(n)^{-1}\int_\Sigma |\nabla u|^2d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}. $$ Next, $$ \begin{aligned} \int_\Sigma|\nabla u|^2d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}&=\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow\infty}\int_{D_\sigma}|\nabla u|^2d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\ &=-c(n)\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow \infty} \int_{D_{\sigma} }R_{\Sigma}u^2d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\partial D_{\sigma} }u\frac{\partial {}u}{\partial {}\eta}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1} \end{aligned} $$ From the expansion of $u$, $E_0<0.$ Therefore $(\Sigma,u^{\frac{4}{n-3} }\overline{g})$ is asymptotically flat with zero amount of curvature, and manifolds with negative energy. Inductively, this creates a contradiction when $n=3$. Therefore we have completed the proof of $E\ge 0$. The article was last updated on 2022-03-07 12:52:49
