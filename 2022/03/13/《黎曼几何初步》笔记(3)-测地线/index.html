exponential mapping -------- ### Geodesic Recall that $\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow The necessary and sufficient condition for M$ to be a **geodesic** is $D_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma=0.$ Therefore, if $\gamma$ is a geodesic, derivation of the inner product shows that $|\dot\gamma|$ is a constant. Therefore, through parameter transformation, we may always assume that it has a unit tangent vector and is parameterized by arc length. Call it a **regular** geodesic. ### Definition Next we introduce the concept of **exponential mapping**. Roughly speaking, The exponential mapping $\exp:M_x\rightarrow M$ at $x\in M$ is defined as follows: Given $v\in M_x,$ Starting from $x$, walk along the geodesic $\gamma_v$ with $v$ as the initial tangent vector to $y,$ So that the arc length from $x$ to $y$ is exactly $|v|.$ then the exponential mapping is defined by $\exp_xv=y$. Intuitively, $M_x$ can be defined in the neighborhood of the origin, but it may not be defined as a whole. It will be shown later that the overall definition of exponential mapping is equivalent to requiring that the Riemannian manifold $M$ is **complete**. The geometric proof of the above intuition is quite clumsy, and it is simpler to explain it in the form of differential equations. Recall that geodesics are determined by a system of ordinary differential equations: $$ \frac{d {}^2\gamma^i}{d {}t^2}+\Gamma^i_{jk}\frac{d {}\gamma^j}{d {}t}\frac{d {}\gamma^k}{d {}t}=0,\quad \,\forall\,i=1,\cdots,n. $$ According to the basic theorem of ODE, $\,\forall\,x\in M,$ $\varepsilon>0,$ Neighborhood $\mathscr{U}\subset M$ of $\,\exists\,x$ and $\delta>0,$ Such that $\,\forall\,y\in \mathscr{U},$ $v\in B_y(\delta)\subset M_y,$ There is a geodesic $\gamma_v,$ with initial tangent vector $v$, and its domain includes $[0,\varepsilon].$ Note that the geodesic after linear transformation of parameters is still a geodesic, so the following proposition is obtained: $\,\forall\,x\in M,$ $\,\exists\,neighborhood of x$$\mathscr{U}\subset M,$ $\omega>0,$ $\,\forall\,y\in \mathscr{U},$ $v\in B_y(\omega)\subset M_y,$ There is a geodesic $\gamma_v,$ with $v$ as the initial tangent vector The domain includes $I=[0,1].$ At this time, $\exp_x:B_x(\omega)\rightarrow M$ defined by $\exp_x v=\gamma_v(1)$ is the exponential mapping that satisfies the aforementioned definition. At the same time, it can be defined on the open set $U:=\mathscr{U}\times B(\omega)$ in the tangent bundle $TM$, Given by $\exp(y,v):=\exp_y v$$\exp:U\rightarrow M.$ It can be seen from the dependence of the solution on the parameters that the exponential map $\exp$ is smooth. ### Properties Below we explain that in small neighborhoods, the exponential map $\exp_x$ is a diffeomorphism. Since $M_x\cong \mathbb{R}^n,$ the tangent space on it is naturally equal to $M_x$ itself. Consider $(d\exp_x)_O:M_x\rightarrow M_x,$ and it is easy to verify that it is an identity mapping. In this way, it is non-singular, so $\exp_x$ is indeed diffeomorphism in the neighborhood of the origin. It is easy to prove its statement on a large scale: **Lemma 1**. *$\,\forall\,K\Subset M,$ $\,\exists\,\varepsilon>0,$ $\,\forall\,x\in K,$ $\exp_x$ is a diffeomorphism on $B_x(\varepsilon)$.* Next, we discuss the local shortest property of the geodesic. In the following, $x is fixed by default, and the subscript of $x$ is omitted. Let $\exp$ be a diffeomorphism on $B(\delta)$. Let $B_\delta:=\exp B(\delta).$ $\,\forall\,v\in B(\delta),$ Call the image of **radial line segment**$c(t):=tv:[0,1]\rightarrow B_\delta$ under exponential mapping $\exp$ to be the **radial geodesic in $B_\delta$.** We will prove the following theorem: **Theorem 2**. *Suppose the piecewise $C^\infty$ curve $\sigma$ connects $x,y\in B_\delta.$ If $\xi$ is a radial geodesic connecting $x,y$, then $L(\sigma)\ge L(\xi),$ is equal if and only if $\sigma$ is a monotonic reparameterization of $\xi$.* We note that this is obvious in Euclidean space, It is proved that it is only necessary to decompose the tangent vector of the curve into a radial component and a spherical tangential component. Obviously it is the shortest when there is no spherical tangent, and the radial component needs to be kept positive. In order to move the proof to the manifold, we need to consider how to obtain the sphere and decompose it. Define vector field $\mathscr{R}:$ in $M_x\setminus\{0\}$ $$ \mathscr{R}(v)=\frac{v}{|v|},\quad v\in M_x\setminus\{0\}. $$ **Lemma 3**. *$|d\exp(\mathscr{R})|\equiv 1$* **Certificate:** $\,\forall\,v\in M_x\setminus\{0\},$ Take $\gamma$ as the normal geodesic in $M$, and $\frac{v}{|v|}$ as the initial tangent vector. Then $\exp(v)=\gamma(|v|),$ $$ |d\exp(\mathscr{R}(v))|=|\dot{\gamma}(|v|)|=|\dot{\gamma}(0)|=1. $$ **Lemma 4** (Gauss' lemma). *$\,\forall\,t<\delta,$ Let $S_t:=\exp(S(t))$ be the geodesic surface with radius $t$ in $B_\delta$, then the radial geodesic is orthogonal to each $S_t.$, that is, $d\exp(\mathscr{R})$ is perpendicular to the tangent space of $S_t$.* **Proof:** Since $\exp$ is a diffeomorphism, the following diffeomorphism can be obtained: $$ F:S^{n-1}\times(0,\delta)\rightarrow B_\delta\setminus\{x\}, $$ Where $S^{n-1}=S(1)$ is the unit sphere in $M_x$. $F(p,t):=\exp(tp).$ For each fixed $t, $ $F$ diffeomorphically maps $S^{n-1}\times\{t\}$ to $S_t.$ Taking $S^{n-1}$ local coordinate system $\{y^i\}, $ $r$ is the natural parameter on $(0,\delta)$, Remember $R=dF\left(\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}r}\right),$ $Y_i=dF\left(\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}r}\right),$ Then we only need to prove that $\left<{}Y_i,R\right>=0.$ $$ \begin{aligned} R\left<{}Y_i,R\right>&=\left<{}D_RY_i,R\right>+\left<{}Y_i,D_RR\right>\\ &=\left<{}D_{Y_i}R,R\right>&D_RY_i-D_{Y_i}R=[R,Y_i]=dF\left[\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}r},\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}y_i}\right], D_RR=0\\ &=\frac{1}{2}Y_i\left<{}R,R\right>=0&|R|\equiv 1 \end{aligned} $$ Therefore $\left<{}Y_i,R\right>$ is constant along the radial geodesic. Since when $z\rightarrow x$, $Y_i(z)\rightarrow 0,$ $\left<{}Y_i,R\right>\rightarrow 0.$ Therefore $\left<{}Y_i,R\right>\equiv 0.$ With these two lemmas, we can imitate the Euclidean space and transfer the proof of the theorem to the manifold. Just decompose the tangent vector of the curve into components on the tangent space of $d\exp(\mathscr{R})$ and $S_t$, Find the length and show that when it only has the upper component of $d\exp(\mathscr{R})$ and remains positive, the curve will be the shortest. In particular, the curve at this time is a monotonic reparameterization of the radial geodesic. It can be seen from this theorem that the shortest line must be a geodesic. Otherwise, the local geodesic can be used to transform the curve into a shorter one. But the converse may not necessarily be true. The geodesic is not necessarily the shortest, for example, consider the geodesics on the sphere and cylinder. Metric completeness ---------- In fact, there may not necessarily be a shortest line on the manifold, such as a disk digging out the center point. This involves the discussion of **completeness** of manifolds. We now introduce **distance space structure** on $M$, Usually also called **metric space structure**. Definition$d(x,y)=\inf_{\gamma}L(\gamma),$ $\gamma$ is the $C^\infty$ curve connecting $x, y$. Assert that $d$ is the distance on $M$. Just prove that when $x\neq y$, $d(x,y)\neq 0.$ This is not obvious, Because the definition involves the lower bound. However, based on the local shortest property of the geodesic, The indefinite bound is locally achievable. Therefore, we only need to explain $y\not\in B_\delta,$ $d(x,y)\ge \delta>0$ is enough, and this is easy to explain. Since the metric $g_{ij}$ is continuously transformed, its minimum and maximum eigenvalues are also, Therefore, there is $\varepsilon|v|^2\le g_{ij}v^iv^j\le \alpha|v|^2.$ on the compact set This can show that the distance space $(M,d)$ is consistent with the topology on the original manifold. The Riemannian manifold $M$ is said to be complete if $(M,d)$ is a complete distance space. **Theorem 5** (Hopf-Rinow theorem). *In a Riemannian manifold, the following conditions are equivalent:* 1. $M$* is complete.* 2. $\,\forall\,x\in M,$ $\exp_x$* is defined on the entire *$M_x$*.* 3. $\,\exists\,x\in M,$ $\exp_x$* is defined on the entire *$M_x$*.* 4. Every bounded closed set in $M$* is compact.* **Certificate:** Let us prove that $(1)\Rightarrow(2)\Rightarrow (3)\Rightarrow (4)\Rightarrow (1),$ But only $(1)\Rightarrow (2), $ $(3)\Rightarrow (4)$ are not so trivial. For $(1)\Rightarrow (2),$ only needs to show that each regular geodesic can extend infinitely. If the geodesic can only extend to $(a,b),$ then by completeness, $x=\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow b}\gamma(t)\in M.$ Then the geodesic can be extended near point $x$. $(3)\Rightarrow (4)$ is more difficult. First consider the existing $\exp_x:M_x\rightarrow M.$ Note $\overline{B}_r:=\exp_x\overline{B(r)},$ then the continuous image of the compact set is also compact. For any bounded closed set $K,$ we only need to show that $\,\exists\,\overline{B}_r\supset K,$ In this way, the closed set of compact sets is also compact. For this reason, we prove the following proposition: $\,\forall\,y\in M,$ *There is a shortest geodesic connection*$x,y.$ so that the bounded set can always be contained by a certain geodesic. $\,\forall\,y\in M,$ Let $d(x,y)=r.$ Take $\delta>0$ so that the exponential map on $\overline{B}_{\delta}$ is a diffeomorphism. Take $x_\delta\in S_\delta=\partial B_\delta,$ so that $d(x_\delta,y)$ reaches the minimum. Then $x_\delta=\exp{\delta v},$ $|v|=1.$ Assert that the geodesic $\gamma(t)=\exp(tv)$ drawn by $v$ satisfies $\gamma(r)=y.$ In this way, the geodesic $\gamma$ is the shortest curve connecting $x, y$, and the proposition is proved. We use the continuity method to prove. Note $A:=\{s\in[0,r]:d(\gamma(s),y)=r-s\}.$ First, $0\in A,$ $A$ is not empty. Secondly, $A$ is of course closed due to continuity. Just prove that $A$ is open, That is, $A=[0,r].$, so it can be deduced that $\gamma(r)=y.$ $\,\forall\,s\in A,$ Consider $\overline{B}_{\delta'},$ at $\gamma(s)$ Make the exponential map on it a diffeomorphism. Take $x_{\delta'}\in S_{\delta'}$ so that $d(x_{\delta'},y)$ reaches the minimum. We want to prove that $d(\gamma(s+\delta'),y)=r-s-\delta'.$ is known $$ d(\gamma(s),x_{\delta'})=\delta', \quad d(\gamma(s),y)=r-s=\delta'+\min_{x'\in S_{\delta'} }d(x',y), $$ Therefore $d(x_{\delta'},y)=r-s-\delta',$ Just say $\gamma(s+\delta')=x_{\delta'}.$ Note $$ d(x,x_{\delta'})\ge d(x,y)-d(x_{\delta'},y)=s+\delta', $$ And connect $x\sim\gamma(s),$ The two geodesics of $\gamma(s)\sim x_{\delta'}$ are spliced together to have an arc length of $s+\delta'.$ Recall that the shortest line must be a geodesic, so this piecewise curve is a geodesic. According to the analytical properties of geodesics, The curve is actually not segmented as a whole. Therefore, the part connecting $\gamma(s)\sim x_{\delta'}$ is the extension of the geodesic at $\gamma(s)$, Thus $\gamma(s+\delta')=x_{\delta'}.$ This completes the argument.
