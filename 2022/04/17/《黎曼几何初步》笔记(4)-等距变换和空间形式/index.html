Introduction ---- Now we begin to study the **space form**, that is, the complete Riemannian manifold* with constant cross-section curvature. We have the following basic results: **Theorem 1**. *$\,\forall\,c\in \mathbb{R},$ $n\in \mathbb{Z}_+,$ $\,\exists\,$The only simply connected $n$-dimensional space form of constant cross-section curvature $c$ under isometric transformation.* The complete proof of the theorem is given in the next chapter. In this section we focus on the existence part. Let the Riemannian metric $g,$ The cross-sectional curvature is $K,$. Then for the positive constant $A,$, the cross-sectional curvature of $Ag$ is $\frac{1}{A}K.$ Therefore, we only need to consider the spatial form with cross-sectional curvature $0,\pm 1$. For $c=0,$ Just take $\mathbb{R}^n$; for $c=1,$, just take the well-known unit sphere $S^n$; for $c=-1,$ It is necessary to obtain the equipment **hyperbolic metric**, that is $$ g=\frac{4}{\left(1-\sum_i \left(x^i\right)^2\right)^2}\sum_i dx^i\otimes dx^i $$ The unit ball $B^n.$ We introduce new concepts and later illustrate the last two conclusions. Full geodesic submanifold ---------- ### Definition Let $M$ be a submanifold of the Riemannian manifold $\overline{M}$, equipped with an induced metric. If any geodesic tangent to $M$ in $\overline{M}$ is all located in $M$, Then $M$ is said to be a fully geodesic submanifold in $\overline{M}$. It can be seen that, Fully geodesic submanifolds are very rare, and almost all Riemannian manifolds do not have any fully geodesic submanifolds. However, a new geometric quantity can be introduced, which is very meaningful. **Lemma 2**. *The necessary and sufficient condition that $M$ is the total geodesic submanifold of $\overline{M}$ is that for any vector field $X,Y,$ in $M$ $D_XY=\overline{D}_XY,$ where $D,\overline{D}$ are the Levi-Civita connections on $M,\overline{M}$ respectively.* **Proof:** First, let $M$ be any submanifold of $\overline{M}$. Define the vector fields $X, Y, $ in $M$ $$ S(X,Y):=D_XY-\overline{D}_XY, $$ $S(X,Y)$ is the vector field in $\overline{M}$ that is only defined on $M$. At this time, For any function $f,$ on $M$ $$ S(fX,Y)=fS(X,Y)=S(X,fY). $$ Therefore, $S:M_x\otimes M_x\rightarrow \overline{M}_x,$ can be defined $$ S(v,w)=S(V,W),\quad \,\forall\,V(x)=v,W(x)=w\in M_x, $$ $V, W$ are any vector fields in $M$ that satisfy the above conditions. From the symmetry of the Levi-Civita connection, we can know that, $$ S(v,w)=S(w,v). $$ Now suppose $M$ is the total geodesic submanifold of $\overline{M}$, assert: $$ S(v,v)=0,\quad \,\forall\,v\in M_x, x\in M. $$ Let $\gamma$ be the geodesic in $\overline{M}$ with $v$ as the initial tangent vector, $\overline{D}_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma=0,$ then $\gamma$ is also the geodesic in $M$, $D_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma=0.$ Therefore $$ S(v,v)=D_{\dot\gamma(0)}\dot\gamma-\overline{D}_{\dot\gamma(0)}\dot\gamma=0. $$ Thus, $$ S(v+w,v+w)=2S(v,w)=0\,\Rightarrow\, D_XY=\overline{D}_XY,\quad \,\forall\,X,Y\in \mathfrak{X}(M). $$ On the contrary, let $S\equiv 0.$ take the geodesic $\gamma,$ in $\overline{M}$ that is tangent to $M$ To illustrate $\gamma\subset M.$, take the geodesic $\xi,$ in $M$ to be the same as the initial tangent vector of $\gamma$. At this time, $$ \overline{D}_{\dot\xi}\dot\xi=D_{\dot\xi}\dot\xi\equiv 0, $$ Therefore $\xi$ is also the geodesic in $\overline{M}$. From the uniqueness, we know that $\xi=\gamma,$ Therefore $\gamma$ does fall in $M$. **Note 3**. *The above $S$ is called the **second basic form** of $M$ in $\overline{M}$, and its discussion will be mentioned in subsequent chapters.* Since the curvature tensor is defined by the Levi-Civita connection, we have the following inference: **Lemma 4**. *Suppose $M$ is a fully geodesic submanifold of $\overline{M}$, $K,\overline{K}$ are the cross-sectional curvature functions of $M,\overline{M}$ respectively, then $\,\forall\,x\in M,$ for any $2$-plane $\Pi\subset M_x,$ $K(\Pi)=\overline{K}(\Pi).$* ### Isometric transformation Let’s look at an example first. Let $\gamma$ be the arc length parameter curve, And there is an isometric transformation $\varphi$ with $\gamma$ as the fixed point set. Then assert that $\gamma$ is a normal geodesic. $\,\forall\,t,$ Let $\xi$ be the geodesic with $\dot{\gamma}(t)$ as the initial tangent vector, Then since the isometric transformation maintains the local minimal property, $\varphi(\xi)$ is also a geodesic, And take $d\varphi (\dot\gamma(t))=\dot\gamma(t)$ as the initial tangent vector. From the uniqueness, we know that $\varphi(\xi)=\xi, $ $\xi$ trajectory is a fixed point set, Therefore $\gamma$ and $\xi$ are the same in the common domain and are regular geodesics. More generally, We have the following conclusions: **Lemma 5**. *The fixed point set of an isometric transformation is a complete geodesic submanifold (not necessarily connected).* **Certificate:** We only need to focus on proving that the fixed point set $M$ of $\overline{M}$ with respect to the isometric transformation $\varphi$ is indeed a submanifold, The instructions that follow are consistent with the previous discussion. Definition $$ \mathscr{F}:=\{v\in \overline{M}_x:d\varphi(v)=v\}, $$ Just state $\exp_x(\mathscr{F}\cap B(\delta))=M\cap B_\delta,$ Among them, $\delta$ is a sufficient miniscule such that $\exp_x$ is diffeomorphism on the sphere. In this way, the coordinate neighborhood on $M$ is established, making it a submanifold. The proof of this point is, Also pay attention to using isometric transformation to maintain geodesics and the uniqueness of geodesics. spatial form -------- ### Unit sphere **Lemma 6**. *The unit sphere $S^n$ has constant cross-sectional curvature $+1,$ $\,\forall\,n\ge 2.$* **Proof:** When $n=2$, the cross-section curvature of $S^2$ is Gaussian curvature, which is known as $+1.$ for $n\ge 3,$ Remember $x\in \mathbb{R}^3,$ $y\in \mathbb{R}^{n-2},$ The isometric transformation $\widetilde\varphi on $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$:(x,y)=(x,-y)$ induces the isometric transformation $\varphi,$ on $S^n$ Let $\{(x,0)\}=S^2$ be the fixed point set. Therefore $S^2$ is a fully geodesic submanifold. According to the lemma, The cross-sectional curvature of the plane tangent to $S^2$ in $S^n$ is the same as the cross-sectional curvature of $S^2$, which is $+1.$ For $S^n,$ there is an isometric transformation between any two two-dimensional tangent planes at any two points, Therefore, it is the spatial form with $+1$ as the constant cross-section curvature. ### Hyperbolic metric **Lemma 7**. *The unit ball $B^n$ equipped with a hyperbolic metric is a complete Riemannian manifold with constant cross-section curvature $-1$.* **Proof:** First discuss the completeness. According to the Hopf-Rinow theorem, We can prove that a geodesic starting from the origin can always extend infinitely, Furthermore, $\exp_O$ is defined on the entire tangent space, so the manifold is complete. Consider the curve $\xi(s)=\left(\frac{e^s-1}{e^s+1},0,\cdots,0\right).$ The calculated $s$ is the arc length parameter, And $\xi$ is the fixed point set of the isometric transformation $(x^1,x^2,\cdots,x^n)\mapsto (x^1,-x^2,\cdots,-x^n)$, Therefore $\xi$ is a geodesic. Furthermore, due to symmetry, geodesics starting in all directions can be extended infinitely. Next, take any tangent plane $\Pi\subset \mathbb{R}^n,$ of $B^n$ at point $p$ Let $E$ be any $3$-dimensional subspace containing $\Pi,p$. Due to symmetry, we might as well assume that $E=\mathbb{R}^3,$ Then $E\cap B^n=B^3$ is the fixed point set of the isometric transformation $(x^1,x^2,x^3,x^4,\cdots,x^n)\mapsto (x^1,x^2,x^3,-x^4,\cdots,-x^n)$, is a fully geodesic submanifold. Therefore, it is only necessary to prove that $B^3$ has a constant cross-section curvature $-1$ under the induced hyperbolic metric. For this purpose, spherical coordinates can be used for calculation. In the case of $n=2$, polar coordinates can be used for calculation. We will later refer to the unit ball equipped with a hyperbolic metric as $H^n.$ ### Geodesic properties Next we consider the properties of geodesics in spatial form. Intuitively, Consider two geodesics starting from a point with a fixed initial tangent vector angle, If $\mathbb{R}^n$ is used as the benchmark, $S^n$ will be closer in comparison, while $H^n$ will diverge. This is an important connection between section curvature and geodesics. Next, we take $n=2$ to express this point strictly. Note that since the case $n=2$ is the total geodesic submanifold of the respective general case, This is also true for general $n$ properties. Now suppose $M=\mathbb{R}^2,S^2,H^2,$ and select the reference point $O\in M,$ Define a geodesic circle with radius $r$: $$ C(r)=\{x\in M,d(0,x)=r\}. $$ When $r$ is sufficiently small, $C(r)$ is the image of the circle with radius $r$ in $M_O$ under diffeomorphism $\exp_O$. Its length measures the divergence rate of the geodesics on $M$ starting from point $O$. Let $c_0(r), c_+(r), c_-(r)$ be the length of $C(r)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2, S^2, H^2$ respectively. Obviously $c_0(r)=2\pi r,$ $c_+(r)=2\pi \sin r.$ The final calculation shows $c_-(r)=2\pi \sinh r.$ They are all homogeneous spaces (the homogeneity of $H^n$ will be explained later), Therefore, the selection of reference point $O$ does not affect the length of $C(r)$. It can be immediately seen from the image that the negative curvature pushes the geodesic apart, And positive curvature brings geodesics together. For any complete two-dimensional Riemannian manifold $M,$, select the reference point $O\in M,$ Let us also consider the length $c(r).$ of the geodesic circle $C(r)$. Let $r$ be sufficiently small, then $$ c(r)=\int_0^{2\pi}\left|d\exp_O\left(\frac{d {} }{d {}\theta}(r,\theta)\right)\right|d\theta. $$ It can be seen that we do not need to specifically consider the influence of $d\exp$ on the radial direction. Let's turn to another object first and come back to this issue later. ### Jacobi Field The so-called $C^\infty$**matrix mapping**, Refers to the smooth mapping $\gamma:[a,b]\times [c,d]\rightarrow M.$ From this we can define two vector fields **$T,U:$ along **$\gamma$** $$ T(\gamma(t,u)):=d\gamma\left(\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}t}(t,u)\right),\quad U(\gamma(t,u)):=d\gamma\left(\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}u}(t,u)\right). $$ Each $u\in[c,d]$ determines a curve $\gamma_u(t)=\gamma(t,u).$ When $0\in[c,d]$, $\gamma_0$ is called the base curve of $C^\infty$ rectangle $\gamma$. If we further assume that each $\gamma_u$ is a geodesic of $M$, Then the matrix mapping $\gamma$ is said to be a **single-parameter geodesic family**$\{\gamma_u\}.$ So we have $$ [T,U]=d\gamma\left(\left[\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}t},\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}u}\right]\right)=0,\quad D_TT=0. $$ Furthermore, $$ D_TD_TU=D_TD_UT=D_TD_UT-D_UD_TT-D_{[T,U]}T=-R_{TU}T. $$ Restricted to the base curve, we get: $$ \ddot{U}+R_{\dot{\gamma}_0U}\dot{\gamma}_0=0. $$ Call the above formula **Jacobi equation**, and call it along the curve $\gamma_0,$ The vector field $U$ that conforms to the above formula is **Jacobi field**. The vector field $U$ is called the **transversal vector field** of $\{\gamma_u\}$, so we get: **Lemma 8**. *The transverse vector field of the single-parameter geodesic family along the base curve is a Jacobi field.* conversely, We will explain later that every Jacobi field along a geodesic is a transverse vector field of a certain single-parameter geodesic family. Below we return to the original discussion. We explain that $d\exp_O\left(\frac{d {} }{d {}\theta}\right)$ is the transverse vector field of a certain single-parameter geodesic family, Therefore, the Jacobi equation gives the decisive connection between curvature and $d\exp_O\left(\frac{d {} }{d {}\theta}\right)$, We can also see the important relationship between curvature and $c(r)$. Let $p\in M_O,$ $X$ be the vector in $M_O$, Then $\Gamma(t,u):=\exp_O\frac{t}{r}(p+uX)$ defines a single-parameter geodesic family, $r=|p|,$ satisfies $$ U(0)=0,\quad \dot{U}(0)=\frac{1}{r}X. $$ To prove the second formula, the coordinate system determined by $\exp_O:B(\delta)\approx B_\delta$ is introduced, It is called **normal coordinate system** and satisfies: $$ \left\{\begin{aligned} &\left<{}\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x^i}(0),\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x^j}(0)\right>=\delta_{ij},&\,\forall\,i,j,\\ &D_{\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x^i}(0)}\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x^j}=0,&\,\forall\,i,j. \end{aligned}\right. $$ The first formula is obvious. For the second formula, define $\beta:M_O\times M_O\rightarrow \mathbb{R},$ $\beta(e_i,e_j)=D_{\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x^i}(0)}\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x^j},$ The linear extension becomes a symmetric bilinear form. Since the radial straight lines in the normal coordinate system are all geodesics, $\beta(v,v)=0,$ So $\beta\equiv 0,$ This gives us the second formula, so in the normal coordinate system, $\Gamma_{ij}^k\equiv 0.$ Now we can come back and explain that $\dot{U}(0)=\frac{1}{r}X.$ Since $X$ is a constant vector, $\Gamma_{ij}^k\equiv 0,$ $$ D_{\dot{\gamma}_0(0)}U=\frac{1}{r}X+\frac{t}{r}D_{\dot{\gamma}_0(0)}X=\frac{1}{r}X. $$ Now taking $X=\frac{d {} }{d {}\theta}(r,\theta)$ shows that $d\exp_O\left(\frac{d {} }{d {}\theta}\right)$ is indeed the transverse vector field of a certain single-parameter geodesic family. Let $\gamma$ be the base curve $\Gamma_0, $ $W(t)$ be the unit parallel vector field along $\gamma$, $W(t)\perp\dot{\gamma}(t).$ For the transverse vector field $U,$ by Gauss's lemma, It is also orthogonal to $\dot{\gamma}(t)$. Therefore $U(t)=f(t)W(t).$ By Jacobi equation, $$ \ddot{f}+Kf=0,\quad f(0)=0,\quad \dot{f}(0)=1. \eqno{(\ast )} $$ Here $K(t)$ is the Gauss curvature at $\gamma(t)$. Applying it to the two-dimensional space form, we get: $$ f(r)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &r, &&K=0,\\ &\sin r, &&K=+1,\\ &\sinh r, &&K=-1. \end{aligned}\right. $$ This is the $c_0,c_+,c_-.$ we asked for previously. The formula $(\ast )$ was discovered by Jacobi, and the initial conditions are called **standard initial conditions**. Due to the curvature-controlled properties of the geodesic, It can be expected that a manifold with sufficient positive curvature should be compact (Bonnet-Myers theorem), The negative curvature manifold makes us expect that the exponential map at a point will be a diffeomorphism (Cartan-Hadamard theorem). Finally, some comments are given. Suppose $\dim M\ge 3,$ say $M$ is **pointwise constant section curvature**, If the cross-sectional curvatures of all $2$-planes at each point on $M$ are the same. The classic **Schur's theorem** means, *A manifold is of point-wise constant cross-section curvature if and only if it is of constant cross-section curvature*. The proof makes use of the second Bianchi identity. This section only discusses the simply connected space form. Other space forms come from geometry, group theory, Topology and other viewpoints are also very important to consider. Their classification is also an important topic.
