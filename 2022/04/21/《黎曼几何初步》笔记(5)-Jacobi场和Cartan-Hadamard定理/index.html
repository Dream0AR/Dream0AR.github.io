Jacobi field -------- Recall that $\{\gamma_s\}$ is a single-parameter geodesic family, and $U$ is a transverse vector field. Then $U$ is a Jacobi field restricted to the baseline $\gamma=\gamma_0$, satisfying the Jacobi equation: $$ \ddot{U}+R_{\dot{\gamma}U}\dot{\gamma}=0. $$ It is a system of second-order ordinary differential equations, so it has the following lemma: **Lemma 1**. *For the geodesic $\gamma,$, for any given $v,w\in M_{\gamma_0},$, there is a unique Jacobi field along $\gamma$, such that $U(0)=v,$ $\dot U(0)=w,$ and the zero points of the Jacobi field along the geodesic are discrete.* For the (unidirectional) lemma in the previous section, we have a more complete proposition: **Lemma 2**. *Given a geodesic $\gamma$ and a vector field $U along it, it is a Jacobi field if and only if $U$ is the restriction of the transverse vector field on $\gamma$ of a single-parameter geodesic family $\{\gamma_s\}$, $\gamma_0\equiv \gamma.$* **Proof:** Just prove that $U$ is the case of Jacobi field. Take the geodesic (or any curve) $\zeta,$ with $U(0)$ as the initial tangent vector Take the parallel vector field $T,W,$ on it to satisfy $T(0)=\dot\gamma(0),$ $W(0)=\dot U(0),$ We are going to knit a family of single-parameter geodesics. Take $\gamma_u(t)=\exp_{\zeta(u)}t(T(u)+uW(u)),$ Then $\{\gamma_u\}$ forms a single-parameter geodesic family, and $\gamma_0\equiv \gamma.$ Remember that its transverse vector field is limited to $\gamma$ as $U_1,$. Just verify that $U_1(0)=U(0),$ $\dot U_1(0)=\dot U(0).$ can be obtained from the following formula, $$ U_1(0)=\left.\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}u}\gamma_u(0)\right|_{u=0}=\dot\zeta(0)=U(0). $$ For the second equation, write $\Gamma(t,u):=\gamma_u(t),$ $T_1:=d\Gamma\left(\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}t}\right).$ Recall $[T,U]\equiv0,$ from the previous section. Similarly we have: $$ \dot U_1(0)=D_{\dot\gamma(0)}U_1=D_{U_1(0)}\dot\gamma=D_{\dot\zeta(0)}T_1. $$ And we know that since $d\exp_O=\mathrm{id},$ $$ T_1(\zeta(u))=\left.\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}t}\exp_{\zeta(u)}t(T(u)+uW(u))\right|_{t=0}=T(u)+uW(u). $$ Finally, since we choose a parallel vector field, we get $$ \dot U_1(0)=D_{\dot\zeta(0)}(T(u)+uW(u))=W(0)=\dot U(0). $$ **Lemma 3**. *Suppose $U$ is the Jacobi field along the geodesic $\gamma$, then there is a Jacobi field $U^\perp$ that satisfies $\left<{}U^\perp,\dot\gamma\right>,$ $a,b\in \mathbb{R},$ such that* $$ U=U^\perp +(at+b)\dot\gamma. $$ **Certificate:** First we can see, $$ D_TD_T\left<{}U,\dot\gamma\right>=D_T\left<{}\dot U,\dot\gamma\right>=\left<{}\ddot U,\dot\gamma\right>=\left<{}-R_{\dot\gamma U}\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma\right>=-R(\dot\gamma,U,\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)=0. $$ Therefore $\left<{}U,\dot\gamma\right>(t)=a't+b'.$ is orthogonalized, let $$ a=\frac{a'}{|\dot\gamma|^2},\quad b=\frac{b'}{|\dot\gamma|^2},\quad U^\perp=U-(at+b)\dot\gamma, $$ Then $\left<{}U^\perp,\dot\gamma\right>=0,$ and $U^\perp$ is indeed a Jacobi field: $$ \ddot{U}^\perp=\ddot U=-R_{\dot\gamma U}\dot\gamma=-R_{\dot\gamma U^\perp}\dot\gamma-(at+b)R_{\dot\gamma\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma=-R_{\dot\gamma U^\perp}\dot\gamma. $$ Therefore, the Jacobi field perpendicular to $\dot\gamma$ is interesting and is called **normal Jacobi field**. Since $\left<{}U,\dot\gamma\right>=at+b,$ As long as the initial value conditions are $\left<{}U(0),\dot\gamma\right>=\left<{}\dot U(0),\dot\gamma\right>=0,$ Then $U(t)$ is the normal Jacobi field. Similarly, we also have the inference: **Corollary 4**. *If $U$ is the Jacobi field along the geodesic $\gamma$, there exists $t_1\neq t_2,$ $\left<{}U,\dot\gamma\right>(t_1)=\left<{}U,\dot\gamma\right>(t_2)=0,$ then $U$ is the normal Jacobi field.* Now we use the Jacobi field to understand $d\exp_x.$. Recall from Chapter 3, We get that $d\exp_x$ is always non-degenerate in the radial direction. Now consider the singularity at $p\in M$, Let $X$ be the tangent vector at point $p$, perpendicular to the radial straight line $t\mapsto tp.$ and take the single-parameter geodesic family: $$ \gamma_u(t)=\exp_xt(T+uX),\quad T=p. $$ Since $U(t)=d\exp_x tX,$ $U(0)=0,$ $\dot U(0)=X,$ $\left<{}X,T\right>=0,$ The limit of the transverse vector field $U$ on the base curve $\gamma=\gamma_0$ is the normal Jacobi field. Since $U(1)=d\exp_x X,$ we can get *Gauss's lemma*, and there are also the following lemmas: **Lemma 5**. *The necessary and sufficient condition for $d\exp_x$ to degenerate at $p\in M_x$ is that there is a normal Jacobi field that is not constant zero on $\gamma(t)=\exp_x tp$, and it is zero at $x$ and $\exp_xp$.* If $d\exp_x$ degenerates at $p\in M_x$, then $p$ is said to be the **conjugate point** of the mapping $\exp_x$, Call $\exp_x p$ the **conjugate point** of $x$ along the geodesic $\gamma(t)=\exp_x tp$. From the lemma just mentioned, we can see that it makes sense to call it "conjugation": this relationship is symmetrical. Application ---- Next we introduce the first **"holistic"** theorem, that is, we only need to do some topology on the entire manifold, Properties that can be established after geometric assumptions. **Theorem 6** (Cartan-Hadamard theorem). *Suppose $M$ is a complete Riemannian manifold with non-positive cross-section curvature, then $\,\forall\,x\in M,$ $\exp_x:M_x\rightarrow M$ has no conjugate points; when $M$ is a simply connected complete Riemannian manifold, if $\exp_x:M_x\rightarrow M$ has no conjugate points, then $\exp_x$ is a diffeomorphism.* **Proof:** Just prove that for any geodesic $\gamma,$ there is a non-trivial normal Jacobi field $U,$ As long as $U(0)=0,$ then $\,\forall\,t>0,$ $U(t)\neq 0.$ Define $f(t)=\left<{}U,U\right>(t),$ then $$ \ddot f=2\left(\left<{}\dot U,\dot U\right>+\left<{}\ddot U,U\right>\right)=2\left(\left<{}\dot U,\dot U\right>-\left<{}R_{\dot \gamma U}\dot\gamma,U\right>\right)\ge 0. $$ The last inequality sign is because $R(\dot\gamma,U,\dot\gamma,U)=K(\Pi)|\dot\gamma\wedge U|^2\le 0,$ $\Pi$ is the subspace formed by $\left<{}\dot\gamma,U\right>$. Therefore $f\ge 0$ is a convex function, and the conclusion is proved by combining the zero-point discreteness of the Jacobi field. In fact, a more precise estimate can be proved: $$ \frac{d {}^2}{d {}t^2}|U(t)|\ge -|U(t)|\cdot |\dot\gamma(t)|\cdot|K(\Pi(t))|\ge 0. $$ From this formula, *Rauch's comparison theorem* can be derived. The second part of the proof only needs to show that $\exp_x$ is a covering map. Since the bottom space $M$ is simply connected, Obtaining $\exp_x$ is an injection, and thus a diffeomorphism. We illustrate this through the following two lemmas. **Lemma 7**. *Suppose $\varphi:M\rightarrow M'$ is a local isometric map, $M$ is complete, then $\varphi$ is a covering map, and $M'$ is also complete.* **Proof:** In addition to using completeness and geodesic properties, the proof is more about topological techniques and will be skipped here. The core is to use isometric transformation to ensure geodesic properties. Explain that locally $\varphi=\exp_{\varphi(x)}\circ d\varphi \circ \exp_{x}^{-1}.$ **Lemma 8**. *Suppose $M$ is a complete Riemannian manifold, $x\in M,$ $\exp_x:M_x\rightarrow M$ has no conjugate points, then $\exp_x$ is a covering map.* **Proof:** Take $g'=\exp_x^\ast g,$ and pull the measure on $M$ back to $M_x$. It is easy to see that the ray starting from the origin is a geodesic in $M_x$ in the sense of $g'$, then based on the completeness of $M$, These rays can be extended infinitely, so according to the Hopf-Rinow theorem, $(M_x,g')$ is complete. Defined by $g'$, $\exp_x:(M_x,g')\rightarrow (M,g)$ is naturally a local isometric mapping, Then we get the conclusion immediately from the above lemma. Now we use Jacobi's theorem to illustrate the uniqueness of the form of a simply connected space. **Theorem 9**. *Suppose $M,M'$ are two $n$-dimensional simply connected space forms, and the cross-sectional curvature is $c.$ Let $x\in M,$ $x'\in M',$ $\{e_i\},\{e'_i\}$ be the standard orthonormal basis in $M_x,M'_x$ respectively. Then there is a unique isometric mapping $\varphi:M\rightarrow M',$ such that $\varphi(x)=x',$ $d\varphi(e_i)=e'_i.$* **Proof:** The proof of the theorem is relatively complicated and will be omitted here. The proof may as well take $c=0,\pm 1,$ $M=S^n,\mathbb{R}^n,H^n$ is enough. The main step is to show that $\varphi=\exp_{x'}\circ\Phi\circ \exp_{x}^{-1}$ is what you want. Among them, $\Phi$ is the only linear mapping such that $\Phi(e_i)=e'_i$. In addition, When $c\le 0$, the Cartan-Hadamard theorem can directly take out such $\varphi,$ When $c>0$, it is necessary to take such $\varphi$ locally and then make appropriate continuation. The following three lemmas are used in the proof. The first two lemmas are used to expand the Jacobi equation, The last lemma illustrates the uniqueness of isometric transformation. **Lemma 10**. *Suppose $M$ is an $n$-dimensional space form, and the cross-sectional curvature is $c.$ Let $x\in M,$ $\{e_i\}$ be the $M_x$ standard orthonormal basis. Then the curvature tensor of $M$ satisfies:* $$ R_{e_ie_j}e_k=c(\delta_{ik}e_j-\delta_{jk}e_i),\quad 1\le i,j,k\le n. $$ **Proof:** Just prove that a curvature tensor is indeed defined according to this formula, And the cross-sectional curvature of this curvature tensor is also $c.$ Then since the cross-sectional curvature determines the curvature tensor, We have established the identity relationship between the two curvature tensors. **Corollary 11**. *The notation is the same as the previous lemma, assuming $v$ is the unit vector in $M_x$, $v^\perp$ is the orthogonal complement of $v$, then* $$ R_{vw}v= \left\{\begin{aligned} &cw, &&w\in v^\perp,\\ &0, &&w=\alpha v. \end{aligned}\right. $$ **Lemma 12**. *Suppose $\varphi_1,\varphi_2:M\rightarrow N$ are two local isometries, satisfying $\,\exists\,x\in M,$ $\varphi_1(x)=\varphi_2(x)=y\in N,$ and $d\varphi_1(x)=d\varphi_2(x):M_x\rightarrow M_y. $ Then we have $\varphi_1=\varphi_2.$* **Proof:** Take $M$ neutron set $$ \mathscr{S}:=\{y\in M: \varphi_1(y)=\varphi_2(y),d\varphi_1(y)=d\varphi_2(y)\}, $$ Just show that it is both open and closed (the connectivity assumption is always the default). The open set is explained using $\exp_y$, Closed sets are described by continuity. Note that our main theorem has a noteworthy corollary. When $M=M'$, we get: **Corollary 13**. *Suppose $M$ is an $n$-dimensional complete simply connected Riemannian manifold, then $M$ is a space form if and only if for any standard orthonormal basis $\{e_i\},\{e_i'\},$ in any $x,x'\in M,$ $M_x,M_x'$, there is an isometric autotransformation $\varphi,$ such that* $$ \varphi(x)=x', \quad d\varphi(e_i)=e_i'. $$ This shows that any simply connected space form must be a homogeneous Riemannian manifold, especially $H^n$ is homogeneous, And this cannot be seen trivially. It can be further explained that these spatial forms are all homogeneous at two points, That is, for any $p_1,p_2,q_1,q_2\in M,$ $d(p_1,p_2)=d(q_1,q_2),$ There is an isometric mapping $\varphi:M\rightarrow M,$ such that $\varphi(p_i)=q_i,$ $i=1,2.$ **Corollary 14**. *All simply connected space forms are homogeneous at two points.* **Proof:** Since the manifold is complete, There are geodesics $\zeta,\xi$ connecting $p_1,p_2;q_1,q_2.$ respectively. Take the isometric transformation $\varphi,$ Make $\varphi(\zeta(0))=\xi(0),$ $d\varphi(\dot\zeta(0))=\dot\xi(0).$ By preserving the properties of geodesics through isometric transformation, we know that the proposition $\varphi(\zeta)=\xi,$ is proved.
