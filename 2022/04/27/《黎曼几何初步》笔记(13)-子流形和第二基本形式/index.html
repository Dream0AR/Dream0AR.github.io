second basic form ---------- Let $\widetilde{M}$ be the $\widetilde{n}$ dimensional Riemannian manifold, $M$ be the $n$-dimensional submanifold, $n<\widetilde{n}.$ Let $\mathscr{N}(M)$ be the entire **normal vector field** of $M$ in $\widetilde{M}$, $\mathscr{T}(M)$ is the entire tangent vector field. In order to examine the appearance of the submanifold, We take the normal vector field $N\in \mathscr{N}(M),$ and consider $\widetilde{D}_XN.$ This is equivalent to examining all $\left<{}\widetilde D_XN,Y\right>,$ $Y\in \mathscr{T}(M).$ This defines the **second basic form**. For the given $\nu\in \mathscr{N}(M),$ define: $$ \Pi_\nu(X,Y)=\left<{}\widetilde{D}_X\nu,Y\right>,\quad X,Y\in \mathscr{T}(M), $$ Call $M$ corresponding to the second fundamental form of $\nu$. **Lemma 1**. *For each fixed $\nu,$ $\Pi_\nu$ is a quadratic symmetric covariant tensor field on $M$.* **Certificate:** Since $\left<{}\nu,Y\right>\equiv 0,$ $Y\in \mathscr{T}(M),$ For any $X,Y\in \mathscr{T}(M),$ we have: $$ 0=X\left<{}\nu,Y\right>=\left<{}\widetilde{D}_X\nu,Y\right>+\left<{}\nu,\widetilde{D}_XY\right>, $$ Therefore we also have the **Weingarten equation**: $$ \Pi_\nu(X,Y)=-\left<{}\nu,\widetilde{D}_XY\right>. $$ Next, Since $[X,Y]\in \mathscr{T}(M),$ $$ \left<{}\nu,\widetilde{D}_XY\right>=\left<{}\nu,\widetilde{D}_YX\right>+\left<{}\nu,[X,Y]\right>=\left<{}\nu,\widetilde{D}_YX\right>, $$ Then from the Weingarten equation, $\Pi_\nu(X,Y)=\Pi_\nu(Y,X).$ In addition, it is easy to see $$ \Pi_\nu(fX,Y)=f\Pi_\nu(X,Y)=\Pi_\nu(X,fY), $$ Therefore $\Pi_\nu$ is indeed a tensor field on $M$. The lemma is proved. It can be seen that $\Pi_\nu(X,Y)(x)$ also only depends on $\nu(x),$ And it is $C^\infty$ linear with respect to $\nu$. Note that $M_x^\perp$ is the orthogonal complement of $M_x$ in $\widetilde{M}_x$, and **quadratic form** can be defined $$ S:M_x\otimes M_x\rightarrow M_x^\perp, $$ make $$ \left<{}S(v,w),\nu\right>:=\Pi_\nu(v,w),\quad \,\forall\,\nu\in \mathscr{N}(M). $$ It is easy to see that $S(v,w)$ is the projection of $-\widetilde{D}_XY(x)$ on $M_x^\perp$, The value of $X(x)=v,Y(x)=w,$ has nothing to do with the selection of vector field. The trace $\sum_{i=1}^nS(e_i,e_i)\in M_x^\perp,$ of $S$ is denoted as $\eta(x),$ $\{e_i\}\subset M_x$ is a standard orthonormal basis. Then $\eta\in \mathscr{N}(M),$ It is called the **average curvature normal vector field** of $M$, and $|\eta|$ is called the **average curvature** of $M$. Sometimes the symmetric linear transformation $A_\nu:M_x\rightarrow M_x,$ is also defined such that $$ \left<{}A_\nu(v),w\right>=\Pi_\nu(v,w)=\left<{}v,A_\nu(w)\right>, $$ That is, $A_\nu(v)$ is the projection of $\widetilde{D}_v\nu$ on $M_x$, We also call $A_\nu$ the **second fundamental form** corresponding to $\nu$. Its relationship with the average curvature is: $$ \left<{}\eta(x),\nu\right>=\sum_{i=1}^n\left<{}S(e_i,e_i),\nu\right>=\sum_{i=1}^n\Pi_\nu(e_i,e_i)=\sum_{i=1}^n\left<{}A_\nu(e_i),e_i\right>=\operatorname{tr}A_\nu. $$ When $\widetilde{n}=n+1$, $M$ is the **hypersurface** in $\widetilde{M}$, At this time, the unit normal vector field $\nu,$ can be locally fixed, denoted as $A:=A_\nu.$ At this time $\widetilde{D}_v\nu\in M_x,$ $A(v)=\widetilde{D}_v\nu.$ The eigenvalues of $A$ are called principal curvatures. They are all real due to symmetry, and are denoted as $p_1,\cdots,p_n.$ $\det A$ is called the **Gauss curvature** of $M$. When $n=2$, it is consistent with the cross-sectional curvature, This is Gauss's **Egregium (brilliant) theorem**. Suppose $\widetilde{M}=\mathbb{R}^{n+1},$ If the hypersurface $M$ is orientable, Then there is an overall unit normal vector field $\nu,$ that can be defined $\Gamma:M\rightarrow S^n,$ $x\mapsto \nu(x),$ is called **Gauss map**. Then, $$ d\Gamma(X)=\widetilde{D}_X\nu=A(X),\quad \frac{\Gamma^\ast \Omega_0}{\Omega}=\det d\Gamma=\det A, $$ Where $\Omega_0,\Omega$ are the volume elements of $S^n,M$ respectively, From this we can see the geometric meaning of Gauss curvature $\det A$. If $M$ is assumed to have positive Gauss curvature, then the Gauss map $\Gamma$ is an immersion. Taking the standard Riemannian metric $g_0,$ on $S^n$ can give $M$ a new Riemannian metric $\Gamma^\ast g_0.$ When $M$ is compact, $\Gamma^\ast g_0$ is complete. Selected by the metric, $\Gamma$ is locally isosome, Therefore, it is a covering map. And since $S^n$ is simply connected, $\Gamma$ is a diffeomorphism. Thus we have the lemma: **Lemma 2**. *Suppose $M$ is a compact hypersurface in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ with positive Gauss curvature, then the Gauss map $\Gamma:M\rightarrow S^n$ is a diffeomorphism.* **Corollary 3**. *The compact hypersurface with positive curvature in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ must be a convex surface, that is, it always falls on one side of its tangent plane.* Now define two orthogonal projections: $$ \top:\widetilde{M}_x\rightarrow M_x,\quad \perp:\widetilde{M}_x\rightarrow M_x^\perp, $$ It is easy to verify that $\top(\widetilde{D}_XY)$ satisfies the properties of Levi-Civita contact on $M$, Therefore $D_XY=\top(\widetilde{D}_XY).$ Recall that we explained earlier that $S(X,Y)$ is the projection of $-\widetilde{D}_XY(x)$ on $M_x^\perp$, Therefore we have: $$ S(X,Y)=\perp(-\widetilde{D}_XY)=D_XY-\widetilde{D}_XY. $$ This is exactly the definition introduced when discussing fully geodesic submanifolds earlier. Now we can say that $M$* is fully geodesic if and only if its second fundamental form is always zero.* The second fundamental form thus characterizes the difference from the full geodesic. Gauss equation ---------- Recall that the cross-sectional curvature of any $2$ dimensional tangent plane of the full geodesic submanifold is the same as the original manifold, For the general case we have the following equation: **Proposition 4** (Gauss equation). *$\,\forall\,X,Y\in \mathscr{T}(M),$ We have:* $$ \left<{}\widetilde{R}_{XY}X,Y\right>=\left<{}R_{XY}X,Y\right>-\left<{}S(X,X),S(Y,Y)\right>+|S(X,Y)|^2. $$ **Proof:** The proof is a straightforward calculation: $$ \left<{}\widetilde{R}_{XY}X,Y\right>=\left<{}\widetilde{D}_{[X,Y]}X,Y\right>-\left<{}\widetilde{D}_X\widetilde{D}_YX,Y\right>+\left<{}\widetilde{D}_Y\widetilde{D}_XX,Y\right>, $$ Among them, since the image of $S$ falls in $M_x^\perp$, $$ \left<{}\widetilde{D}_{[X,Y]}X,Y\right>=\left<{}D_{[X,Y]}X,Y\right>-\left<{}S([X,Y],X),Y\right>=\left<{}D_{[X,Y]}X,Y\right>, $$ $$ \begin{aligned} \left<{}\widetilde{D}_X\widetilde{D}_YX,Y\right>&=\left<{}\widetilde{D}_X(D_YX-S(X,Y)),Y\right>\\ &=\left<{}\widetilde{D}_XD_YX,Y\right>-\left<{}\widetilde{D}_XS(X,Y),Y\right>\\ &=\left<{}D_XD_YX,Y\right>-\left<{}S(X,D_YX),Y\right>-X\left<{}S(X,Y),Y\right>+\left<{}S(X,Y),\widetilde{D}_XY\right>\\ &=\left<{}D_XD_YX,Y\right>+\left<{}S(X,Y),D_XY-S(X,Y)\right>\\ &=\left<{}D_XD_YX,Y\right>-\left<{}S(X,Y),S(X,Y)\right> \end{aligned} $$ Similarly, $$ \left<{}\widetilde{D}_Y\widetilde{D}_XX,Y\right>=\left<{}D_YD_XX,Y\right>-\left<{}S(X,X),S(Y,Y)\right>. $$ The conclusion can be obtained by integrating the above equations. **Note:** The Gauss equation describes $\top\widetilde{R}_{XY}Z,$ And $\perp \widetilde{R}_{XY}Z$ will be given by the following **Codazzi equation**. In fact, Using the same method used to prove the Gauss equation, we can prove that the following equation holds: $$ \left<{}\widetilde{R}_{XY}Z,W\right>=\left<{}R_{XY}Z,W\right>-\left<{}S(X,Z),S(Y,W)\right>+\left<{}S(X,W),S(Y,Z)\right>. $$ **Corollary 5**. *Suppose $M$ is a hypersurface in $\widetilde{M}$, and $A$ is the second fundamental form of the unit normal vector field $\nu$. If $P$ is the $2$-plane in $M_x$, let $A|_P:P\rightarrow P$ be $A:P\rightarrow M_x$ and then composite the orthogonal projection $M_x\rightarrow P,$ then* $$ \widetilde{K}(P)=K(P)-\det(A|_P). $$ **Certificate:** When $M$ is a hypersurface, Since $\left<{}S(X,Y),\nu\right>=\Pi_\nu(X,Y)=\left<{}A(X),Y\right>,$ $S(X,Y)=\left<{}A(X),Y\right>\nu.$ Therefore, take $\{e_1,e_2\}$ as the standard orthonormal basis of $P$, rule $$ \left<{}S(e_1,e_1),S(e_2,e_2)\right>-|S(e_1,e_2)|^2=\left<{}A(e_1),e_1\right>\left<{}A(e_2),e_2\right>-\left<{}A(e_1),e_2\right>^2=\det(A|_P). $$ Since $\widetilde{K}(P)=\left<{}\widetilde{R}_{e_1e_2}e_1,e_2\right>,$ $K(P)=\left<{}R_{e_1e_2}e_1,e_2\right>,$ The conclusion is obtained immediately from the Gauss equation. **Corollary 6** (Gauss's wonderful theorem). *Suppose $M$ is a surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$, then its cross-sectional curvature is equal to its Gauss curvature.* **Corollary 7**. *Suppose $\{\nu_1,\cdots,\nu_{\widetilde{n}-n}\}$ is the unitary basis of $M_x^\perp$, then $\,\forall\,X,Y\in M_x,$* $$ \left<{}\widetilde{R}_{XY}X,Y\right>=\left<{}R_{XY}X,Y\right>-\sum_i \Pi_{\nu_i}(X,X)\Pi_{\nu_i}(Y,Y)+\sum_i \Pi_{\nu_i}(X,Y). $$ **Corollary 8** (Synge Lemma). *Assume $M$ contains a geodesic $\widetilde{M}$$\gamma,$, then for any tangent plane $P$ satisfies $\dot\gamma\in P\subset M_x,$ we have $K(P)\le \widetilde{K}(P).$* **Proof:** Suppose $P$ is formed by $\dot\gamma,Y$. Since $\gamma$ is a geodesic, $S(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)=0.$ Then let $X=\dot\gamma,$ in Gauss equation Get to the conclusion immediately. **Corollary 9**. The **ruled surface** (single parameter straight line family) in *$\mathbb{R}^3$ has non-positive Gauss curvature.* **Corollary 10**. *Suppose $M$ is a $2$-dimensional surface in $\widetilde{M}$, including a geodesic $\gamma,$ of $\widetilde{M}$, then $K(M_{\gamma(t)})\equiv \widetilde{K}(M_{\gamma(t)})$ if and only if $M_{\gamma(t)}$ is parallel to $\gamma$ in $\widetilde{M}$.* **Proof:** Suppose $\gamma$ is a regular geodesic, $X$ is the unit vector field in $M$, and is perpendicular to $\gamma$, then $$ \widetilde{K}(M_{\gamma(t)})=K(M_{\gamma(t)})+|S(\dot\gamma(t),X(t))|^2. $$ Therefore the equality holds if and only if $\widetilde{D}_{\dot\gamma}X\equiv D_{\dot\gamma}X.$ $\dot\gamma$ is of course parallel to $\gamma$ in $M,\widetilde{M}$, and $$ \left<{}D_{\dot\gamma}X,\dot\gamma\right>=\dot\gamma\left<{}X,\dot\gamma\right>-\left<{}X,D_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma\right>=0, \quad \left<{}D_{\dot\gamma}X,X\right>=0\:\Rightarrow\: D_{\dot\gamma}X=0, $$ $X$ is parallel along $\gamma$ in $M$. Therefore, the equation is always true if and only if $X$ is also parallel along $\gamma$ in $\widetilde{M}$, That is, $M_{\gamma(t)}$ is parallel to $\gamma$ in $\widetilde{M}$. **Corollary 11**. *Suppose $M$ is a Riemannian manifold, $x\in M.$ takes $\{x^i\}$ as the normal coordinate system of $x$, $P\subset M_x$ is formed by $\{\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x^1},\frac{\partial {} }{\partial {}x^2}\}$, $\mathscr{S}$ is the surface $\{x^i=0:i\ge 3\}.$, then the cross-sectional curvature of $P$ is equal to the Gauss curvature of $\mathscr{S}$ under the induced metric.* **Proof:** Just note that for the normal coordinate system, $S\equiv 0 at point $O$, $ the second fundamental form is zero, Then it is obtained from the Gauss equation. Codazzi equation ---------- Codazzi equation is also called Codazzi-Mainardi equation, By directly calculating, we can get the following results about $\widetilde{R}_{XY}Z$. **Proposition 12** (Codazzi equation). *If $X,Y,Z\in \mathscr{T}(M),$ $R,\widetilde{R}$ is the curvature tensor of $M,\widetilde{M}$, then* $$ \begin{aligned} \perp\widetilde{R}_{XY}Z=&\left(\perp \widetilde{D}_XS(Y,Z)-S(D_XY,Z)-S(Y,D_XZ)\right)\\ &-\left(\perp \widetilde{D}_YS(X,Z)-S(D_YX,Z)-S(X,D_YZ)\right) \end{aligned} $$ The Codazzi equation and the Gauss equation are often collectively referred to as the **Gauss-Codazzi equation**. The Codazzi equation is generally complex, but is simpler in special cases. **Proposition 13**. *Suppose $\widetilde M$ is a constant curvature space, $M$ is the hypersurface therein, then $(D_X\Pi)(Y,Z)$ is symmetric about $X,Y,Z$, where $X,Y,Z\in \mathscr{T}(M).$* **Proof:** Since $\Pi(Y,Z)=\Pi(Z,Y),$ is obviously symmetric about $Y,Z$. So we only need to prove $$ (D_X\Pi)(Y,Z)=(D_Y\Pi)(X,Z). $$ Taking the unit normal vector field $\nu,$ then $S(X,Z)=\Pi(X,Z)\nu.$ Therefore $$ \perp\widetilde{D}_YS(X,Z)=\perp\left((Y\Pi(X,Z))\nu+\Pi(X,Z)\widetilde{D}_Y\nu\right)=(Y\Pi(X,Z))\nu. $$ When $\widetilde{M}$ has constant cross-section curvature $c$, $$ \widetilde{R}_{XY}Z=c(\left<{}X,Z\right>Y-\left<{}Y,Z\right>X). $$ This is because the right formula constitutes the curvature tensor on $\widetilde{M}$, and also has constant cross-section curvature $c.$ At this point you can find $\perp\widetilde{R}_{XY}Z=0.$ Substituting into the Codazzi equation, we have: $$ Y\Pi(X,Z)-\Pi(D_YX,Z)-\Pi(X,D_YZ)=(D_Y\Pi)(X,Z)=(D_X\Pi)(Y,Z)=\cdots. $$ A fundamental application of the Gauss-Codazzi equation is to illustrate the existence and uniqueness of a hypersurface given its second fundamental form. The core of the proof is to consider a system of overdetermined first-order partial differential equations determined by: $$ S(Y_i,Y_j)=\Pi(Y_i,Y_j)\nu=D_{Y_i}Y_j-\widetilde{D}_{Y_i}Y_j,\quad \widetilde{D}_{Y_i}\nu=A(Y_i). $$ Since the equation is overdetermined, it may not have a solution. In fact, the necessary and sufficient condition for solvability is to satisfy the previous Gauss-Codazzi equation, This is obtained by considering **Frobenius theorem**. Below we only describe the theorem of existence and uniqueness. **Theorem 14** (Uniqueness). *Suppose $M,M'$ are two hypersurfaces in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$, with unit normal vector fields $\nu,\nu'.$ Let $\varphi:M\rightarrow M'$ be isometry, such that $\Pi(X,Y)=\Pi'(d\varphi(X),d\varphi(Y)),$ Then $M, M'$ are **contract**, That is, there is an isometric isomorphism $\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{n+1},$ such that $\Phi_M=\varphi.$* **Theorem 15** (Existence). *Given that the $n$ Veriemannian manifold and the symmetric tensor $\Pi,$ satisfy the Gauss-Codazzi equation, then $M$ can be locally isometrically embedded in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$, so that its second basic form relative to a certain unit normal vector field is exactly $\Pi. $ Furthermore this embedding is unique in the sense of a phase-difference global isometric isomorphism $\overline{\varphi}:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$.* We can generalize the theorem to $n+1$ Viriemannian manifolds $\widetilde{M},$ But the form of the Gauss-Codazzi equation will be more complex. In addition, it can also be extended to cases with higher codimension, But at this time, additional integrability conditions **Ricci equation** need to be added, It is obtained by considering the $\perp\widetilde{R}_{XY}\nu$ term. Ricci equation ---------- Take $\{\nu_i\}$ as the standard orthonormal basis of $M_x^\perp$, Definition **Basic form**$\beta_{r}^s(X_p)=\left<{}\widetilde{D}_{X_p}\nu_r,\nu_s\right>.$ Denote $\Pi_r:=\Pi_{\nu_r},$ take $\{U_i\}$ as the standard orthonormal basis of $\{M_x\}$, Define the convolution between the second basic forms: $$ \Pi_r\ast \Pi_s(X,Y)=\sum_i \Pi_r(X,U_i)\Pi_s(U_i,Y), $$ It is easy to see that this equation can be defined reductively as a convolution between quadratic symmetric tensor fields.
